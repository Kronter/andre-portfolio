  text: |-
    I can picture a crew switching Styles in the middle of a performance. I can also picture myself halfway through a turn, trying to remember whether I was supposed to flip a card. I wanted the first of those quite a bit more than the second.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    [NOTE]***Quick note:*** Deferred does not mean discarded forever. I am looking at what the streamlined prototype needs now.[/NOTE]
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I ran into a version of that problem three times, first with **Gear**, then with **Styles** and **Sequences**. Each sounded like something a crew ought to have. Finding room for it during a Jam was another matter.
- type: heading-2
  text: "Gear had a job, but not enough presence"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    An earlier version of the game included Gear or Equipment. I liked the possibility of giving players persistent tools and a recognisable play identity. In theory, this type of card could support different strategies without rewriting the whole resource economy.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    In practice, my later design notes recorded a less flattering result: Equipment was confusing or underused. A mechanic can sound central in a design document and barely register during play. When that happens, adding stronger effects may make players notice it, but it doesn't necessarily solve the underlying problem.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Was Gear something players actively planned around? Did it influence the decisions I cared about most? Or was it an extra object they were expected to remember while concentrating on Maneuvers and resource totals? Those questions mattered more than how many interesting pieces of Gear I could design.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The direction changed toward Styles: a clearer expression of a performer's identity and approach. That was a meaningful shift in purpose, not just a new name for the same equipment slot.
- type: heading-2
  text: "A Style should change expression, not just statistics"
- type: image
  src: "/Styles.png"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The richer Styles concept developed two sides, [SIDENOTE]Verse and Chorus[/SIDENOTE], along with conditions for shifting between them. I liked the idea of a performer moving through different phases of a Jam, gaining a recognisable rhythm rather than simply accumulating passive bonuses.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    It's a compelling fantasy. A player establishes a pattern, builds toward a transition and changes how their Maneuvers feel. Styles could give decks personality while leaving the underlying resource contest recognisable.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    But I had to distinguish the fantasy from the implementation. A Style that flips between modes is not automatically engaging. Players need to know when it changes, what choices it opens and why the change feels earned. If most of the cognitive effort goes into checking the trigger, the feature may be delivering less than it promises.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    That's true even when the feature is sophisticated and technically functional. What matters is whether the additional structure changes what the player [VIOLET]*wants*[/VIOLET] to do.
- type: heading-2
  text: "Sequences were another tempting connection"
- type: image
  src: "/Sequences.jpg"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Sequences explored a different idea: the order in which Maneuvers appear on the Line could create combo-like patterns. This matched the performance theme nicely. A sequence of moves could express rhythm and composition, much like a routine assembled step by step.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The difficulty was that the rules initially relied too heavily on players recognising boundaries in the sequence. During competitive play, that created uncertainty about when a Sequence started, when it ended and which cards belonged to it. The physical game had no automatic system to repair the ambiguity.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    One idea was to announce the Sequence and fan those cards together. Then you could point at the whole thing and ask me about it, even if you hadn't seen me play the first card.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    We eventually tried a game with both Styles and Sequences left out. I thought I'd miss having a way to express the crew's style. During that test, at least, I was quite happy choosing my next Maneuver.
- type: heading-2
  text: "A feature can be deferred without being rejected"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    One of the habits I am trying to strengthen is keeping separate records for ideas that failed, ideas that are incomplete and ideas that simply aren't necessary yet. Those categories imply different next steps.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Gear was historically superseded by Styles. Sequences evolved through several attempts to improve readability. Styles and Sequences were then deferred from the streamlined prototype, while remaining potential advanced layers. Treating all of those as the same kind of deletion would erase important information about the design.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    This is particularly relevant to card games because future sets and formats can create legitimate reasons to revisit an idea. But I don't want that possibility to become an excuse for carrying its cost now. 'Maybe useful later' is a perfectly good reason to archive a mechanic. It is not, by itself, a good reason to make every new player learn it.
- type: heading-2
  text: "What am I asking you to remember?"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I increasingly think about a player's attention as part of the game's economy. Every new state, keyword, timing step and component asks the player to notice something. Sometimes that investment produces a rich decision. Sometimes it simply makes the rules longer.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I test that trade by asking what happens if I remove a mechanic and leave the rest of the game intact. Does a meaningful choice disappear? Does the game lose a recognisable emotional beat? Or does play become smoother while almost nothing valuable is lost?
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    For Styles and Sequences, the simplified tests were a strong reason to focus on the Maneuver-resource-Jam loop first. I could always test whether one advanced layer improved a specific weakness once the basic game was legible and interesting.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    If I put one of these back in, I'd start with just that one and use much the same deck. If I change the cards as well, I'll have a hard time telling what we actually enjoyed.
- type: heading-2
  text: "Design for a readable first experience"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The other reason I value keeping the core small is accessibility. I don't mean removing every difficult decision. I mean letting a new player understand what a decision is *about* before asking them to optimise it. A few clear, interacting systems can support plenty of mastery.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I still like the idea of players developing a signature performance through Style changes and sequences of moves. I just don't think every attractive part of the fantasy has to enter the first version at once.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    [BLOCK]My aim is to create enough depth that players begin noticing possibilities I didn't explicitly teach them. When a subsystem gets in the way of noticing those possibilities, it can be better to set it aside. The design isn't less ambitious because the prototype has fewer rules. The ambition has moved from the number of components to the quality of their relationships.[/BLOCK]
