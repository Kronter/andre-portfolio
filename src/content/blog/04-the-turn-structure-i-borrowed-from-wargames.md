- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    My partner quite enjoys a complicated board game. What she enjoys less is spending a turn knocking someone else's pieces off the board. That distinction mattered when I started thinking about how a turn in my TCG should work.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I still wanted us to have reasons to watch each other play. If I can plan my whole turn while you take yours, how much of a game are we actually playing together?
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    My first prototype had a problem here. The resource systems could be interesting on their own, but that didn't guarantee that the experience of taking turns was interesting [VIOLET]*together*[/VIOLET].
- type: heading-2
  text: "Watching isn't the same as participating"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Think about a traditional turn in which I perform a long series of actions, resolve several effects, build my position and finally hand over control. The other player may be watching carefully, but their decisions are delayed until I've finished making mine.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    That structure works for many games. It also creates a risk if the core appeal is meant to be a back-and-forth performance or rivalry. I wanted each action to give the other player something to consider before the situation moved too far away.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    My question became: what is the smallest unit of action that creates an interesting response?
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Notice that I'm not asking how often a player can interrupt. Interruption systems carry their own problems [SIDENOTE]timing windows, priority rules and extra explanations[/SIDENOTE], as well as the uncertainty of whether you should ask your opponent to stop. I wanted to see whether the rhythm of ordinary turns could do some of that work first.
- type: heading-2
  text: "What I borrowed from skirmish games"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    One thing I enjoy in skirmish games is **alternating activations**. I move a piece, then you get to do something with yours. By the time it comes back to me, the move I was saving might look considerably less appealing.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The part I wanted to borrow was the few seconds after the other player acts. I’ve been lining up a move, they do something I hadn’t counted on, and now I get to decide whether my plan is still any good. They can create that moment for me while leaving every piece I own on the table.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I explored bringing that cadence into the card game. During a Jam, players would alternate playing Maneuvers, building toward a shared resolution. The opponent would not have to wait through an entire private engine turn before deciding how to respond.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    A resource decision could now be both an economic choice and a public signal. Even a relatively simple move might shift what the other player expects or needs next.
- type: heading-2
  text: "Timing is part of game feel"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    We often talk about game feel in terms of movement, animation, audio or the weight of an action. In a tabletop game, timing itself creates a feeling. Frequent meaningful turns can make play feel like a conversation; long isolated turns can make it feel like a presentation.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Neither is automatically wrong. For this project, though, the fantasy I was moving toward was competitive performance. I wanted the sense of building something together, trying to outdo one another and reading the other person's intent as the Jam developed.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Alternating actions supported that fantasy more naturally than a long series of private turns. It also created an interesting restriction on design space: each individual action needed to matter enough to justify giving the opponent a turn.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    That was a useful constraint. If a card's only purpose was to maintain an invisible engine and nothing changed for anyone else, I had a reason to question it.
- type: heading-2
  text: "Indirect interaction still needs stakes"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    There is a trap here: frequent turns do not automatically produce frequent interaction. Two players can alternate actions every thirty seconds and still ignore each other. The game needs shared stakes.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    For Tracers, the emerging shared stakes were the resources both players were competing over and the final comparison at the end of a Jam. I might care about the resource I was generating, but I also needed to notice which resource you were building. A public contest turns an ordinary efficiency question into a contextual one.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    We still had moments in later tests where we took turns quite happily and barely looked at each other's cards. That's what got me interested in letting a player predict which resource their opponent would go for next. Those cards still need testing.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    That distinction matters because the first experiment answered one question—would shorter alternating turns produce a better rhythm?—without answering every question about player interaction.
- type: heading-2
  text: "What does the waiting player get to do?"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Borrowing an idea from a skirmish game didn't make my card game a skirmish game. It gave me a different way to examine agency, timing and the space between actions.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    It also reinforced my belief that looking outside your usual medium is one of the fastest ways to challenge an assumption. A designer working entirely inside TCG conventions might ask which instant-speed cards encourage interaction. Looking at alternating activations let me ask whether the base turn structure could encourage it instead.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    [BLOCK]If I want to know whether the shorter turns are helping, I'd watch the player who's waiting. Is there a move I could make that would change the card they're about to play?[/BLOCK]
