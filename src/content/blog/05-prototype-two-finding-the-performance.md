- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    At some point I stopped looking at a pile of TCG experiments and started seeing a performance. That was when this project began to feel like [VIOLET]Tracers[/VIOLET].
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    [NOTE]***Quick note:*** This is a look back at Prototype Two. Some scoring and character rules changed in later iterations.[/NOTE]
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Players were taking turns making commitments, responding to one another's progress and building toward a shared resolution. They weren't commanding armies or reducing health bars. The interesting part was the momentum of the exchange itself.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    So I began thinking of it as a performance.
- type: heading-2
  text: "What if competition wasn't combat?"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The direction that became **Tracers** imagines rivalry through movement, rhythm, style and showmanship. Think of an underground competitive scene with the energy of street performance and sport rather than a fantasy duel. There can still be tension, risk and outplaying the opponent. The players simply aren't trying to hurt one another as the central objective.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I liked the thematic possibilities because they connected to my original social goal: a competitive experience that could create excitement without leaning on direct attack as its main language.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    More importantly, the theme gave the mechanics a purpose. A turn could feel like adding a move to a performance. A round could feel like a Jam. Resource pressure could represent qualities of that performance rather than a pile of anonymous mana.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The theme wasn't a coat of paint applied after the fact. It helped me ask whether the rules were expressing the experience I wanted.
- type: heading-2
  text: "A Jam is a shared argument"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    In a recognisable version of Prototype Two [SIDENOTE]before the later scoring revisions[/SIDENOTE], players take alternating Maneuver turns within a **Jam**. A Maneuver is a commitment to the developing performance. When both players are done, the Jam is resolved by comparing three resource totals: **Flow, Hype and Momentum**.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Each resource contest can award a Point; a tie awards nothing in the documented model. The game therefore creates several related contests rather than one simple number that always matters more than the rest.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    That structure creates decisions you can explain in ordinary terms. Do I strengthen a category I'm already winning, or contest one that's close? If my opponent appears committed to one direction, can I make another more valuable? Is the card worth playing now, or does it have greater value for what comes next?
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Those are the sorts of questions I wanted the game to provoke. The current direction still needs balancing work, but the core relationship is clear: the things players produce are also the things they compete over.
- type: heading-2
  text: "Flow, Hype and Momentum aren't just labels"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    In the documented design, the three resources form a triangular generation relationship: one type of Maneuver feeds another resource, which then feeds the next. This creates an interesting separation between the kind of action you choose and what it makes possible.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The resource triangle illustrates why I wanted these systems to reinforce one another. By connecting generation to visible competitive goals and alternating actions, the same choice could support planning, interaction and the performance fantasy.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    That doesn't mean triangular systems are inherently deep. If one route is consistently strongest, the apparent choice disappears. If the outputs are difficult to read, the system becomes arithmetic without personality. The point is to have a relationship worth testing, not a clever diagram worth defending.
- type: heading-2
  text: "A supporting cast, not a new game on top"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    As the project developed, I explored other identity systems: Styles, chained Sequences, and the Tracers who represent the people performing. Some ideas added flavour and meaningful possibilities. Others demanded more explanation than the current prototype could support.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    An earlier version used a Featured Tracer and rotation linked to winning or losing. Later design work moved toward three Tracers assigned to Flow, Hype and Momentum, each building toward an Entrance. That makes the player's resource decisions do another job: they influence which performer can join the action. The precise balance and wording of individual abilities are still being developed.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The useful design question is not whether a Tracer ability sounds exciting. It is whether the moment of an Entrance fits the pace of a Jam and rewards understandable choices. When several resources accelerate an Entrance too quickly, or players cannot tell when it happens, the surrounding connections need work.
- type: heading-2
  text: "The table is part of the interface"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Prototype Two also exposed a problem digital designers can sometimes postpone. Who can see which effect is active? Where is card order recorded? How does someone tell what a resource produces without reading a paragraph of text?
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The table doesn't remember or calculate anything for us. If a Sequence starts or ends without a visible marker, somebody has to hold the state in their head. If a resource total changes, the counters and the card layout have to make that clear. Even a mechanically correct system can feel opaque when its state is hard to inspect.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I started treating the physical arrangement as a designed interface. Information needed to live somewhere visible, not merely exist in the rules document. This lesson would eventually feed back into the way I thought about digital UI and player-facing feedback.
- type: heading-2
  text: "Finding the identity is not finishing the game"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Prototype Two gave me something the first prototype hadn't: a coherent direction in which the mechanics, turn rhythm and theme could reinforce one another. It did not eliminate balance problems, unclear wording or the need to remove features.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    [BLOCK]In fact, later playtests made several of those problems more obvious. That is exactly what I want a more focused prototype to do. Once it has an identity, I can judge new systems against that identity instead of asking whether each system is fun in isolation.[/BLOCK]
