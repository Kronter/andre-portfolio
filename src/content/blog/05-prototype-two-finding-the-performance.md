- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    What would these cards look like if I could step inside the game for a minute? One player makes a move, someone else answers, and both are trying to get a crowd on their side. I could picture that. It looked a bit like a dance battle.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Up to that point, I'd been looking at cards as contributions to three resource totals. Giving those cards a physical performance to represent changed how I thought about the whole game. It gave the players a reason to respond to each other, a reason for a crowd to care, and a reason for the city itself to look different afterwards.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I keep coming back to the same image: two rival crews have finished a Jam, the crowd is still arguing about who won, and you can look up and see what they made together. Even the crew that lost can point to part of it and say, 'That bit was ours.'
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    [NOTE]***Quick note:*** This brings together how Prototype Two found its performance identity and the world that grew from it. Some details below came in later iterations; the scoring, character and Style systems continued to change after the original prototype.[/NOTE]
- type: heading-2
  text: "I wanted competition, not another fight"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The first motivation was closer to home than the worldbuilding. I wanted a competitive card game I could enjoy with people who like strategy but don't always enjoy direct confrontation. My partner is quite comfortable with complicated games, but she's less interested in spending the evening attacking someone else's pieces or repeatedly stopping them from doing anything.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I didn't want that to mean two people quietly playing solitaire next to one another. I still wanted rivalry. I wanted players watching each other's moves, adjusting a plan, committing to a risk, sometimes bluffing and sometimes getting in the way. I just wanted the conflict to have a different shape.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I looked beyond other TCGs for that shape. In a breaking battle, one dancer's move gives the next dancer something to answer. Wrestling can be as much about drawing a reaction from the crowd as overpowering an opponent. Capoeira can be playful, cooperative and fiercely competitive in the same exchange. Skating and other street sports have their own mixture of individual expression, shared spaces and people watching for the next extraordinary thing.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Those references helped me imagine a match that had real pressure without making injury or destruction the central objective. Instead of asking who had dealt the most damage, I could ask whose performance had the better [VIOLET]**Flow, Hype and Momentum**[/VIOLET].
- type: heading-2
  text: "From playing cards to tracing a city"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    That idea became **Tracing**: an underground sport in a layered, near-future magitech city. Rival crews move through streets, walls, rooftops and other spaces using skating, parkour, dance, acrobatics and equipment that lets them move in ways we can't quite manage in real life. The performers are **Tracers**. The moves they perform are **Maneuvers**, and their competitive exchanges are **Jams**.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I don't want Tracing to be defined by inline skates alone. A Tracer might use rune-powered shoes, parkour gloves that grip impossible surfaces, jumping stilts or a customised belt that gives them another burst of motion. What matters is how their equipment, personality and movement style change the way they travel through the environment.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The world sits somewhere between recognisable urban culture and fantasy technology. I like seeing a familiar jump, rail or wall and imagining what a small push from technology or magic could make possible. Magitech—[SIDENOTE]technology modified or powered by magic and runes[/SIDENOTE]—lets me exaggerate movement without turning it into anonymous beams of energy.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    And I wanted an identity that felt specific to this city. The layered streets, crews, clothing, improvised spaces and counterculture should feel lived in. References such as *Air Gear*, *RKGK* and *Kemuri* help me think about kinetic movement and vertical urban spaces; *Dirty Pair* and *Record of Lodoss War* remind me of the bold character silhouettes and colour I enjoy in older animation. I like the theatrical side of wrestling and the energy of hip-hop, breaking and street art too. Those are influences, not a request to turn Tracers into a copy of any one anime, sport or aesthetic.
- type: heading-2
  text: "The street is the canvas"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The part of the setting that really made it click for me was the mark a crew leaves behind. In the world concept, their movement produces traces: paint, light, arcane marks or other visible lines that record where they've been. A crew might cross an entire street in a sequence of jumps and spins, leaving an impossible shape behind them.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Now imagine two crews sharing that space. The first takes a route along a wall; the second answers by crossing over it or cutting through the middle. Their marks overlap. By the end, the two teams have competed to outstyle one another, but they've also created one piece of movement art together.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    That isn't a rule saying the players must literally draw a picture on the table. It's a way for me to picture what their cards are doing. The outcome belongs to the winner, but the performance belongs to both crews. The crowd can remember the individual moments as well as the result.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    It's also a useful question to ask of the art: from the other side of the street, can you tell who moved where, how they reached a rooftop and which teammate is about to answer? I'd rather see a sense of motion and intention than a character standing still surrounded by special effects.
- type: heading-2
  text: "A Jam should play like a conversation"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Once I could picture Tracing as a sport, the existing turn structure felt more natural. Prototype Two used alternating Maneuver turns. I make one move, then you answer. We build a Jam in front of both of us rather than each taking a long, isolated turn.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    A card doesn't just advance my plan. It becomes part of an exchange. If I put pressure on one category, you can decide whether to contest it, let me have it, or set up a different direction. Each player has a reason to pay attention while the other acts.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    In the recognisable Prototype Two structure [SIDENOTE]before later refinements to timing and scoring[/SIDENOTE], a Jam ended when the players were done taking actions. We compared **Flow, Hype and Momentum** separately. The player ahead in each resource earned a point for that category; a tied category awarded no point. So a Jam could be decided across three smaller contests rather than by a single health bar.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I liked the decisions this created. Do I make the part of my performance that's already strong even stronger, or try to close the gap somewhere else? If you're committed to Hype, should I chase it or make sure I'm ahead in Flow and Momentum? Can the move I play now help me afford the next one?
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The core idea was that the things the performers produce are also the things the players compete over. That let the rules and the fiction reinforce each other instead of sitting beside one another.
- type: heading-2
  text: "What the three resources feel like"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The names mattered because they gave me a language for what was happening in the Jam. **Flow** suggests continuity and control—finding a rhythm, concentrating, making a sequence of movements feel intentional. **Hype** is the response from the crowd, the flourish or unexpected trick that pulls everyone's attention. **Momentum** is what carries the action forward, physically and competitively, even after something dramatic changes the pace.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I don't treat those as rigid categories of movement. A big move might be fluid, spectacular and fast all at once. The terms are there to suggest aspects of a performance, not to impose three disconnected types of choreography.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Mechanically, the resources also connect through a triangle. Playing a **Momentum Maneuver generates Hype**; a **Hype Maneuver generates Flow**; and a **Flow Maneuver generates Momentum**. Maneuvers have costs too, so playing one can both consume resources and generate others. A player can plan a route through the triangle rather than simply play the same type to build the same score.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The relationship isn't automatically interesting just because it makes a neat diagram. If one route is always best or if it's too hard to see what each move generated, the triangle isn't doing its job. But when the resource decision also affects what I can play, what my opponent might do and which part of the Jam I'm competing for, it has a better chance of earning its place.
- type: heading-2
  text: "A crew needs people, not just an economy"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The world also gave me a reason to care about the characters doing the performing. I didn't want Tracers to feel like anonymous units in a spreadsheet. A crew has personalities, preferred ways of moving, equipment, rivalries and the possibility of a signature moment.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    An early character approach revolved around a **Featured Tracer**, with rotation connected to match outcomes. Later I explored having three Tracers associated with Flow, Hype and Momentum, building toward an **Entrance** as the player develops that resource. On the street, that can look like a teammate watching the Jam and recognising the exact moment to join in.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I like the idea that progression is visible. If a performer is nearly ready to enter, the other player can see it coming and decide whether to respond. But the rules for thresholds, timing and abilities still need to earn their place mechanically. A strong image in my head isn't evidence that a particular version is balanced.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The same applies to **Styles** and **Sequences**. I explored ways to give each crew a distinctive approach and make particular chains of moves matter. They sound appropriate for a sport about expression. But later streamlined playtests showed that the core Jam could still feel good without some of those layers. The fantasy of a unique crew can survive while a subsystem is deferred or removed.
- type: heading-2
  text: "The table has to show the performance"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Making this as a physical game also kept exposing the practical questions hidden inside a good idea. If the order of Maneuvers matters, where does that order live? If a Sequence begins or ends, can the other person see it? If a resource total changes, can players tell which card changed it without asking me to reconstruct the turn?
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    A digital game can remember, calculate and highlight all of that automatically. The table can't. Someone has to arrange the cards, move the counters and recognise what the new state means. I began treating that arrangement as part of the interface, not a separate concern to solve after the rules.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    That was another reason the performance metaphor helped. A Jam happens in front of spectators. If I want people to enjoy reacting to the performance, the important parts ought to be visible from the other side of the table too. The same design habit transfers back to digital games: a system is only as useful to the player as the decisions they can actually see and understand.
- type: heading-2
  text: "The world has to change what I design"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I'm still interested in who lives in different parts of this city, where crews practise, how neighbourhood identities develop and what an underground league might look like. I don't want to settle every part of that world before I know what the game needs.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    What I do have is a direction that helps me judge new ideas. Does this Maneuver suggest a recognisable action? Does the opposing crew have a reason to watch it? Can the card's resource effects feel connected to what the performer is doing? Can a Tracer's identity come through without requiring another page of bookkeeping?
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Those questions are much more useful than asking whether the world sounds cool on its own. A setting should give me better design decisions. If a rule or piece of art doesn't help convey the exchange between crews, I should be willing to rethink it.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Prototype Two gave me something the first prototype hadn't: mechanics, turn rhythm and a world that seemed to be pointing towards the same experience. It didn't eliminate all the balance and readability problems, and it didn't mean every feature I'd imagined belonged in the final game.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    But I could finally explain what I wanted players to feel. They weren't simply spending cards to win three resource contests. They were crews using a city as a stage, answering each other's moves, drawing in a crowd and shaping an event neither could make alone.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    [BLOCK]I keep returning to the pavement after a Jam. Two crews have gone, the street is covered in their traces, and someone in the crowd can point to where one crew answered the other's move. If the cards can make that relationship feel real, then the world and the game are doing their jobs together.[/BLOCK]
