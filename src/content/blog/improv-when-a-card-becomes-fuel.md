- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I liked the idea of Maneuvers generating the resources that let you play other Maneuvers. It made the economy feel like part of the performance. But the first version of that idea exposed a problem pretty quickly.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Tracers uses three linked resources: a Momentum Maneuver generates Hype, Hype generates Flow, and Flow generates Momentum. That gives you routes to plan through, but it also means the resources you have and the cards you've drawn don't always line up.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I could have a hand of interesting cards and still not have a useful move to make. Maybe I'm holding the right kind of Maneuver to generate Flow, but I can't afford to play it in the first place. Having a good hand shouldn't always mean sitting there waiting for the economy to let you participate.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    [NOTE]***Quick note:*** The conversion amount changed during development. I'm describing why I added Improv, how it evolved, and the tradeoffs I wanted it to create—not claiming that every early version used the current rule.[/NOTE]
- type: heading-2
  text: "A way to keep the Jam moving"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I introduced [VIOLET]**Improv**[/VIOLET] to give players more flexibility when their resources and their hand didn't match. The basic idea was to **discard a Maneuver to gain resources**. You could trade a card you weren't able—or weren't ready—to play for a better chance of doing something useful now.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    It solved two things I cared about. First, the triangle could still encourage players to plan ahead without completely trapping them when they drew the wrong mix of cards. Second, I didn't need to bring back a separate resource deck just to make sure people had something they could do.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    That also fitted the idea of Tracing. A crew shouldn't always follow the perfect routine. Sometimes the move you planned doesn't work, and you adapt in the middle of the Jam.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    But I didn't want Improv to be a free reset button. If you can always turn an unwanted card into exactly what you need, the triangle loses much of its tension. There should be a price to that flexibility.
- type: heading-2
  text: "One card, two possible futures"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    A Maneuver in my hand represents something I could perform later. Using it for Improv means giving up that possibility for resources now. That's a more interesting decision than simply asking whether I have enough fuel.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Do I keep the strong card and try to build towards it? Or do I discard it so I can play another Maneuver straight away? The answer depends on what I'm trying to achieve during this Jam and what I think the other crew might do next.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    There's also the cost of losing a card from my hand. The more often I improvise, the fewer options I may have later. I wanted the mechanic to make a stuck hand more useful without turning every card into an interchangeable battery.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    That was where Improv began doing more than fixing a resource shortage. It was giving players agency when the draw was awkward, but also asking them to weigh short-term tempo against future possibilities.
- type: heading-2
  text: "Why the amount mattered"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    An early version of Improv produced a single resource. It was simple to explain and a useful starting point, but it didn't make the printed cost of the discarded card especially important. Later I changed the rule so Improv would grant the resource symbols represented by that card's printed cost.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Now the cards carry another layer of identity. A more expensive card can provide more immediate fuel, but sacrificing it may mean giving up a more ambitious action. That introduces a tension worth exploring: should I use this powerful card now as a resource, or keep it for the moment when I can actually perform it?
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    It also made the cost printed on a Maneuver matter in two different contexts. One is what I must pay to perform it. The other is what I could gain by giving that performance up. I liked the relationship, although it meant watching how quickly the economy could accelerate when high-cost cards were converted.
- type: heading-2
  text: "Timing is part of the choice"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Once discarding a card could unlock a play, **when** players were allowed to Improv mattered almost as much as the amount they received.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    If I can convert cards whenever I like, I can keep changing what I'm capable of while the other player is trying to follow the Jam. That might suit a different game, but I wanted the back-and-forth to stay readable. Each crew should have a chance to see what the other has just done.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    So I gave Improv a clearer place in the turn: on your own turn, before your Maneuver. The Improv itself doesn't use the turn's Maneuver action. Then you play your Maneuver, resolve it and pass the turn. Discarding a card through Improv doesn't count as playing that card or trigger its normal played effects.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    That boundary matters for pacing. You can adapt when you're stuck, but you still have to commit to an action. The opponent sees the resources you gained and the Maneuver you performed before deciding how to respond.
- type: heading-2
  text: "What the fix taught me"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Improv started as a fairly practical fix: the resource triangle made some hands difficult to use, and I wanted players to keep participating. It didn't stay only a safety valve. By asking players which Maneuver they were willing to lose, it added opportunity cost. By letting them act now at the expense of later options, it shaped pacing. And by setting a clear window for that choice, it kept some agency for the person watching from the other side of the table.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Of course, the details still need tuning. If converting a card is always the right move, the choice isn't doing much. If Improv is too weak, the original problem comes straight back. The goal isn't to make every hand perfect. It's to let players make an interesting decision even when the hand isn't perfect.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    [BLOCK]I added Improv because I didn't want the resource system to stop people playing. What made me keep it was that the solution created its own decisions: what am I giving up, what can I do now, and what will that mean for my next turn?[/BLOCK]
