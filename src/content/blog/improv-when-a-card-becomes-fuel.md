- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    One of the questions that pushed my card game forward was deceptively ordinary: what happens when a player draws a card they cannot, or do not want to, play? In many card games, that card waits. Sometimes waiting is a deliberate investment; sometimes it feels like the game has temporarily taken a choice away. I wanted to see whether the card could remain useful without making every decision automatic.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    That question eventually became *Improv*: a way to discard a Maneuver for resources instead of playing it as a Maneuver. It sounds like a straightforward conversion. The interesting part is what it does to the relationship between your hand, your timing and the things you are trying to achieve.
- type: heading-2
  text: "One card, two futures"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    A card in your hand can represent a future action: something you hope to do when the right moment arrives. If you can also turn it into resources, that future has a price. Spending it now means giving up whatever it might have done later. Keeping it means accepting that you may have fewer resources right now.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I like that kind of tension because it doesn't require an additional board or another deck. The opportunity cost sits in an object the player already understands. You are not being asked to learn a separate minigame before the actual game can happen. You are deciding which version of your plan matters more.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Of course, flexibility isn't automatically meaningful. If the same card is always the correct discard, or if a big card is always the best resource, the apparent decision can collapse into a routine. That is why I think a conversion mechanic has to be evaluated alongside the cards, their costs, and what resources allow players to do.
- type: heading-2
  text: "Why the amount mattered"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    An early version of Improv produced a single resource. It was simple to explain and a useful starting point, but it didn't make the printed cost of the discarded card especially important. Later I changed the rule so Improv would grant the resource symbols represented by that card's printed cost.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Now the cards carry another layer of identity. A more expensive card can provide more immediate fuel, but sacrificing it may mean giving up a more ambitious action. That introduces a tension worth exploring: should I use this powerful card now as a resource, or keep it for the moment when I can actually perform it?
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The solution also creates risks. If drawing extra cards gives a player more possible actions *and* more potential resource fuel, card draw becomes doubly valuable. And once resources also determine when Tracers enter the performance, a large Improv can advance more than one subsystem at the same time.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I didn't want to respond to that automatically by imposing a hard limit. A powerful interaction isn't necessarily a broken one. It depends on whether the other player has ways to respond, whether the timing creates interesting choices, and whether the resulting pace still feels right.
- type: heading-2
  text: "Timing is part of the mechanic"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Another question was when players could Improv. If a player can convert cards at any moment, the resource economy becomes harder to read. The opponent has less ability to predict what's possible, and the table needs to keep track of more potential interruptions.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    For the streamlined rules, I settled on a cleaner boundary: Improv happens on your own turn before you play a Maneuver. Playing the Maneuver ends that turn. The discard produces resources, but it does not count as playing the discarded card and does not activate its normal played effects or generation.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    This is a useful example of a rule whose value isn't just balance. It clarifies the cadence of play. One player acts, the other observes the resulting public state, and then the next player has an opportunity to respond. The system remains flexible without demanding a general out-of-turn response window.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I am careful about calling that timing a final solution to every interaction problem. Different cards could still need specific wording and exceptions. But establishing a clear default makes the exceptions much easier to assess.
- type: heading-2
  text: "The surprise was progression"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    As Tracers evolved, each player gained three characters waiting outside the main deck, each linked to one of the three resources. They entered when their relevant resource reached the printed requirement. That meant Improv no longer affected only what I could play; it could also affect how quickly I brought my characters into the performance.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The combination is exciting. A card that is too expensive to play now may instead help one of my Tracers arrive. But it can also accelerate progression in unexpected ways, especially alongside draw effects. Later playtests exposed exactly this concern: cost-based Improv could enable several Entrances more quickly than originally expected.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The important distinction is between an observation and a verdict. Fast Entrances aren't inherently wrong. They may be dramatic and satisfying. The question is whether they preserve decisions, counterplay and reasonable competitive outcomes. That is why later validation work compared possible Tracer tuning and Maneuver-economy tuning rather than silently changing the Improv rule.
- type: heading-2
  text: "What I would actually test"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    If I were isolating Improv, I wouldn't begin by asking whether a deck feels balanced in general. I'd record when players Improv, which printed costs they discard, which resources they gain, how many cards remain in hand, and whether those gains trigger Entrances. Then I'd ask the players why they made each discard.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I would particularly watch for two failure modes. First, an obvious conversion where the player doesn't have to think. Second, a compounding loop where the player who is already ahead can draw more, generate more and get more advantages without facing a comparable sacrifice.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Those are different problems, and they suggest different changes. One might require a better spread of card roles; the other might require adjustments to draw, resource generation or the timing of character benefits. Adding a blanket restriction before identifying the problem risks destroying the interesting choice as well.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Improv started as a way to make hands more flexible. It became a way to examine opportunity cost, pacing, player agency and the links between systems. That is the sort of evolution I want from a prototype: a small rule makes the right questions more visible. The next challenge was making sure those decisions mattered to the person sitting across the table.
