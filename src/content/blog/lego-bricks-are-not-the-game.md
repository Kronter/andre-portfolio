- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I often explain how I think about game design with [VIOLET]LEGO bricks[/VIOLET]. I like collecting the pieces, but the interesting part is what happens between them.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    [NOTE]***Quick note:*** The game examples are hypothetical. I use them to explain a relationship, rather than as evidence from a finished project.[/NOTE]
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    A game designer collects pieces over time [SIDENOTE]mechanics they've played, systems they've built, patterns they've studied, mistakes they've made and behaviours they've watched players invent[/SIDENOTE]. A resource economy is a piece. A turn structure is a piece. Progression, feedback, pacing, social pressure and player goals are pieces too.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Having more pieces is useful. Knowing how they connect is what makes the collection valuable.
- type: heading-2
  text: "A familiar brick can make an unfamiliar game"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Imagine taking a drafting mechanic, a seasonal board structure and a simple spatial rule. None of those is novel in isolation. Put them together in the right way, and each draft decision can affect not only what you gain, but where it will fit, which spaces it will change and what options become available later.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The same principle works in action games. A movement ability, a cooldown and an environmental hazard are familiar. But if the ability lets players redirect the hazard, a simple traversal tool becomes a combat and positioning tool as well. That new relationship can be more interesting than adding another button.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I don't think originality means inventing a mechanic no one has ever seen. Often it means discovering a useful relationship that changes what the player can experience or express.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    This is one reason I enjoy moving between digital games, board games and tabletop RPGs. They reveal different uses for familiar pieces. A procedure that is mundane in one genre might produce a new kind of decision in another.
- type: heading-2
  text: "Connection isn't just combination"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    There is a weaker version of this idea: take two successful mechanics, put both in the game, and hope that makes the design richer. It often makes the design busier instead.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    When I evaluate a combination, I ask what relationship it creates. Does one system change the player's understanding of the other? Does a decision in one system create a consequence in the other? Do the mechanics reinforce the same fantasy, or compete for attention?
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Consider a small adventure game that combines gathering, changing weather and an environment that visibly responds to the player. Gathering is familiar. Weather is familiar. Environmental reactions are familiar. But if a storm changes which ingredients grow, and those ingredients change how the player prepares for the next journey, each system gives the others a reason to exist.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    That relationship might create depth without needing three entirely separate activities to manage. It might also create new problems: perhaps certain materials become too abundant, or one forecast makes every other decision obvious. The connection is valuable only if it produces worthwhile choices in play. It's an idea to prototype, not a guarantee of good design.
- type: heading-2
  text: "Start with what the player does and feels"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I have an affinity for the playful, interaction-led way I associate with Shigeru Miyamoto's game design: finding pleasure and possibility in what a player can actually do, rather than treating a long feature list as the point of the exercise.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I don't mean that as a claim that I follow a particular Miyamoto formula. It's a way of describing what I prioritise. I want the core interaction to be worth repeating, and I want the systems around it to make that interaction more expressive.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I find a useful point of overlap in Miyamoto's description of games encouraging players to experiment and then responding to what they try. In a [2015 interview about Mario Maker](https://www.tpr.org/science-technology/2015-06-19/q-a-shigeru-miyamoto-on-the-origins-of-nintendos-famous-characters), he connects player creativity with a cycle of trying things and receiving feedback. That resonates with me more than any particular camera, genre or genre convention: the player should be an active participant in discovering what's possible.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    A sophisticated economy that delays every interesting action is not successful just because its numbers are elegant. A simple action can be deeply satisfying if the feedback, timing and consequences make it feel good. Likewise, an accessible game doesn't have to sacrifice mastery. A small, understandable set of pieces can create a wide space for skilful decisions when the relationships are strong.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The question isn't 'how many mechanics have I built?' It's 'what experience emerges from them?'
- type: heading-2
  text: "Give each brick a job"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    One habit I've developed is trying to state a system's job without naming the system. Instead of 'we need a crafting menu,' I might say 'players should regularly decide whether to use a rare material now or save it for something more ambitious.' Instead of 'we need a combo meter,' I might say 'I want sustained skilful play to build anticipation before a payoff.'
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    That language matters because it separates intent from implementation. If the crafting menu turns into busywork, I can test a different way to express sacrifice and preparation. If the combo meter encourages rote behaviour, I can test other feedback patterns. The goal stays visible even while the bricks change.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    It also makes conversations with programmers, artists and UI designers more productive. We can discuss what the player needs to understand and feel before treating the first proposed feature as the only solution.
- type: heading-2
  text: "Remove pieces to reveal the structure"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    This is the part I find hardest. I like ideas. I like elegant interactions. It's easy to protect a subsystem because I can see its potential. But potential isn't enough. If the player can't notice the relationship or if the cost of understanding it is too high, the mechanic might be obscuring the stronger design underneath.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Imagine an exploration game with detailed weather, equipment durability, hunger and camping. All four systems could contribute to a compelling survival fantasy. But if players spend more attention maintaining meters than deciding where to explore, I would first test the experience with one or two layers removed. Which choices disappear? Which become clearer? The answers matter more than the sophistication of the original systems.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Sometimes removing a piece makes the structure more visible. Sometimes it reveals a missing connection that does need a new piece. Either way, I want the decision to come from what the player actually experiences, not from how proud I am of the feature.
- type: heading-2
  text: "Build a library of relationships"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    My personal design toolkit isn't just a catalogue of mechanics. It's a growing memory of relationships: which ones created interesting tradeoffs, which ones created friction, which ones were difficult to explain, and which changed completely when placed in a different context.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    [BLOCK]That's why I think making many small experiments matters. Each one adds more than a mechanism to the collection. It adds an observation about how mechanisms behave together.[/BLOCK]
