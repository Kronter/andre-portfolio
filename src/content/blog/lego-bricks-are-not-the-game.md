- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I tend to think about game design a bit like building with LEGO.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    At first, it's an obvious comparison. You have different pieces. You put them together, move them around, take something apart, swap one piece for another and see what you can build. That flexibility is a big part of how I like to design mechanics and systems.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    But the part of LEGO I find most interesting isn't simply that the pieces are modular. It's how they connect. A pile of individually useful bricks isn't a particularly strong structure. The way you overlap them, support one part with another and build on top of what is already there makes the whole thing work.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    For me, those are two sides of the same design philosophy: [VIOLET]**systems should reinforce one another without becoming impossible to change.**[/VIOLET] And the more I think about learning game design, the more I see the same idea applying to the skills and experiences I build along the way.
- type: heading-2
  text: "A brick should still be a brick"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I like thinking about mechanics as parts I can work on individually. Each one should have a purpose I can explain. I should be able to examine what it contributes, change how it works, replace it with a different solution or remove it if the game no longer needs it.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    This isn't the same as wanting every mechanic to work in isolation. Games are built from relationships. A movement system affects the kinds of spaces you can design. Progression changes how players value their actions. Economy, pacing and difficulty can all influence one another. I want those connections. I just don't want every small change to demand that I rebuild the entire game.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    There's a practical difference between knowing a system depends on another one and having the two so tangled together that I can't tell where one responsibility ends. If I change the way movement uses stamina, I expect that to affect balance and maybe level design. I don't want it to unexpectedly require rewriting every progression rule before I can test the new movement.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Thinking in pieces encourages me to give systems clearer jobs. What is this mechanic for? What information does it need? What does it produce that other systems care about? And what would happen if I tried a different implementation?
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Being able to replace a piece makes experimentation less intimidating. I can hold on to the experience I'm trying to create while questioning whether the specific rule I've written is the right way to achieve it.
- type: heading-2
  text: "Overlapping bricks make a stronger wall"
- type: image
  src: "/Lego.png"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    If you stack LEGO bricks in separate vertical columns, you can build upwards. But if each column stays separate, they don't do much to support each other. Offset the next row so one brick crosses two underneath it, and now it helps hold both together. The structure gets its strength from the overlaps.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    That's the part of the metaphor I keep coming back to.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    In a game, systems design, level design, UX, narrative, economy and balancing don't sit in completely separate towers. A change to one can give another part meaning. A mechanic determines what information a player needs. The way a level presents that information influences which decisions the mechanic creates. Narrative can help explain why the player cares about the action in the first place.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I find it much more interesting when a mechanic does its own job and also contributes to something around it. Maybe the way a player moves changes how they explore, how they approach danger and how they notice opportunities. Maybe a progression reward doesn't just increase a number, but changes how an existing action interacts with the environment.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    That kind of overlap can create [VIOLET]**more interesting decisions from the systems that are already there**[/VIOLET]. The goal isn't necessarily to give players more individual mechanics to learn. It's to let familiar mechanics take on new meaning because of what they're connected to.
- type: heading-2
  text: "Connected doesn't have to mean inseparable"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    There's an apparent contradiction here. If I want all these systems to support one another, how can I also expect to remove or replace one of them?
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I think the answer is in *how* they're connected. When bricks overlap, they reinforce the structure, but they're still recognisable pieces. You can remove one, see what it was supporting and rebuild that part without having to melt the whole thing down.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    That's what I try to aim for with game systems. I want to know which relationships matter and which implementation details are negotiable. A system might be important because it creates a particular choice, supports a feeling or connects two other mechanics. If its current implementation isn't working, I can preserve that purpose while trying a different piece.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Some changes will have a large impact. If I remove a central movement mechanic, of course the spaces and encounters built around it may need redesigning. Modularity doesn't mean pretending those dependencies don't exist. It means making them understandable enough that I can change the design deliberately instead of discovering a knot of accidental dependencies.
- type: heading-2
  text: "The foundation is part of the philosophy too"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The LEGO idea doesn't stop at the game I'm building. It's also how I think about becoming a better designer.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    A strong structure needs a good foundation, but I don't see that foundation as a set of isolated skills that I finish learning one by one. Systems design is one piece. UX is another. So are level design, narrative, economy, balancing and prototyping. My software engineering background contributes to how I think about structure and implementation. Experience from sport and other practical disciplines contributes to how I approach practice, feedback and improvement.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Those aren't separate boxes I only open when a task has the corresponding label. The useful part is often what happens between them.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Knowing more about UX changes how I evaluate a system. Understanding economies changes what I notice in progression. Learning about level design makes me think about how a mechanic fits the spaces where players use it. A new perspective can reinforce something I already knew, or expose a weakness in the foundation I hadn't noticed.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I don't need to become an expert in every discipline. But I do want enough understanding to recognise their relationships. The stronger and broader that foundation becomes, the more ways I have to approach an unfamiliar design problem.
- type: heading-2
  text: "Sometimes learning sideways builds upwards"
- type: image
  src: "/Going Sideways.png"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I can keep practising the things I already know how to do. That absolutely has value. Going deeper into a discipline is often exactly what I need.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    But if I always choose familiar problems and solve them using familiar tools, I can end up reinforcing the same small part of my foundation. Something outside that area may be what helps it grow.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    A different genre can force me to think about player motivation in a new way. Another platform may challenge assumptions about controls or interface. Programming can make me more aware of how cleanly a design can be changed. Sport and other hands-on activities remind me that understanding an idea isn't the same as being able to apply it instinctively.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    That distinction matters to me. I can read a good explanation and agree with it, then still repeat the same mistake later. Practical experience makes me confront the idea. I try something, see where it fails, reflect, adjust and try again. Over time, that knowledge becomes part of how I actually design.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    And because the bricks overlap, what I learn in one area can strengthen another. I'm not starting from zero every time I explore something new. I'm adding to the structure, sometimes from a direction I wouldn't have expected.
- type: heading-2
  text: "How I try to build"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    When I start designing something, I try to keep both parts of the metaphor in mind. First, I want to understand the pieces: what each mechanic is responsible for, what decision or experience it supports, and whether I could change it without losing sight of its purpose.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Then I look at the joins. Which systems reinforce one another? Does a new rule give an existing action more meaning? Does the player understand that relationship? Have I created a useful connection, or another separate thing to manage?
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    As the design grows, I revisit those questions. A mechanic that made sense early on might stop fitting. A better solution might appear after I understand a neighbouring discipline or try an unfamiliar approach. I want to be able to rearrange the structure rather than defend every original brick.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    [BLOCK]I want to build games from pieces that are clear enough to change, connected enough to strengthen one another, and flexible enough that a better idea can take the place of an old one. And I want to keep building my own foundation the same way.[/BLOCK]
