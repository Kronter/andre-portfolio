- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    If you give me two rows of LEGO bricks and ask me to build a wall, the interesting bit is where I put the first brick in the second row. Line it up exactly with the one underneath and I can keep building upwards quite happily. Offset it a little and now it holds two of the lower bricks together. That overlap is the bit I have in mind when I talk about game design.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I like the modular part of the comparison too. I can take a feature apart, replace one piece and see what else moves. But when I picture what I’m building over the course of my career, there’s more in that box than mechanics. There’s what I know about systems and what I’ve learned about talking to a player through an interface. There’s experience designing for mobile, playing tabletop RPGs, building prototypes and balancing an economy. I’m interested in where those things meet, because that’s usually where an interesting design problem turns up.
- type: heading-2
  text: "Where does a systems problem end?"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Take a rule I’ve actually wrestled with. A series of cards played on the table can form a Sequence. From a systems perspective I need to know which card starts it, what extends it and where it ends. I can sit down with the rules and answer all three. Now sit on the other side of the table halfway through a game. Which card started this Sequence?
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    If I have to reach across and point it out, I’ve just found a UX problem inside my systems work. I could write a more precise definition. I could also try moving the cards so you can see the group for yourself. Those changes ask different things of the player. One asks you to learn the rule well enough to reconstruct what happened. The other gives us both something we can point to while we work out what to do next. Actually playing with the cards helped me see the difference.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The overlap can run in the other direction too. In my talk I showed two ways I’d described the move that finishes a performance. I’d used the phrase ‘last card you play’. I also tried calling it a *Closer*. Once I think of someone finishing their routine in front of a crowd, I have a word for the role that move plays. The setting gave me a handle on a wording problem. I still need a precise rule for when it applies.
- type: heading-2
  text: "A change in one part of a game travels"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Imagine a level designer moving a valuable resource from the main path to a side route. On the map it looks like a small edit. From the economy side, a balance calculation that assumed everyone picked it up is suddenly questionable. From the player’s side, it matters whether they could see the resource before choosing the path. I’d want to walk that route before I touched the reward value. Otherwise I might spend an afternoon adjusting the price of something players simply walked past.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    That’s one reason I value having some experience outside the area I’m working in. I don’t need to be the strongest level designer in the room to ask where players first see that side route. I do need enough understanding of someone else’s work to recognise that their answer could change mine. It makes for a much better conversation than turning up with a request to make the reward bigger.
- type: heading-2
  text: "What came back with me"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Making a physical game gave me practice I could bring back to digital work. On a screen the game can remember what happened three turns ago even if nobody playing noticed it. At a table that information has to live somewhere. Sometimes I want it on a card. Sometimes the useful discovery is that I’ve asked both people to remember something neither of them enjoys using.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Later, at work, I was asked to design an event or offer that would get more players to engage. I ended up recreating part of it physically. I wanted to see how the choice in front of the player related to the goal they were working towards. I went through several versions and let go of ideas earlier than I otherwise would have. My recent practice with paper was useful on a feature that players would eventually experience entirely on a screen.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    That’s what I mean by a foundation. The time I spent making something readable across a table is now part of how I think about a digital interface. The time I spend understanding an economy changes what I notice walking through a level. I can keep going deeper into systems design, which I enjoy, and sometimes the best thing I can do for that work is spend a while learning somewhere else.
