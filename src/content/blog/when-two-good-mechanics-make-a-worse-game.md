- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I had a resource system I liked. I had a zone system I liked. I had a main deck full of things I wanted players to do. Each made sense when I looked at it on its own. Then I put them together on a table and discovered that the game was becoming much harder to play than any one of those systems suggested.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The interesting part wasn't that one mechanic was obviously terrible. I could explain why I'd added each one. The problem was what they were asking of the player **together**. They competed for attention, created new procedures and limited the space I had left for the cards that were supposed to be the main attraction.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    That experience reinforced something I now try to remember when designing systems: [VIOLET]**two good mechanics don't automatically make a better game**[/VIOLET]. Their relationship matters at least as much as their individual quality.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    [NOTE]***Quick note:*** I'm talking about the fit between systems, not arguing that every game should have fewer mechanics. A deliberately intricate game can make learning those connections a major part of its appeal.[/NOTE]
- type: heading-2
  text: "A mechanic can work and still be wrong for this game"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    When I'm reviewing a feature, there's a tempting question: does it work? It matters, of course. If the mechanic is confusing, broken or unrewarding, I have something concrete to fix. But I can answer yes to that question and still have a problem.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    A resource loop might create interesting efficiency choices. A progression system might give players something to anticipate. A special action might feel brilliant to use. None of that tells me whether the three belong in the same experience.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Say the resource loop rewards carefully saving for later, while the progression system rewards spending everything as quickly as possible. That could create a good tension. It could also result in two sets of incentives pulling players in directions that feel unrelated. The distinction depends on whether players understand the conflict and get an interesting decision from it.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I'm interested in the difference between a system that **adds another activity** and a system that changes the meaning of an activity already there. The second can sometimes create more depth with less explanation. It isn't guaranteed, but it's usually worth looking for.
- type: heading-2
  text: "The cost isn't only another rule"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Every mechanic uses some of the game's budget. There's development time and balance work, but also player attention, memory, pacing and the number of interactions every future addition must account for.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    That last cost is particularly easy to underestimate. If I add a mechanic that affects resources, I don't only have to make that mechanic work. I have to reconsider every card or ability whose cost it can change. If I add a new timing window, I may have to reconsider everything that can happen during that window. One neat feature can turn a straightforward design space into a much narrower one.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    That's what happened with my early resource deck. It was supposed to let me make interesting decisions about resources. Instead, so much of the game's utility and efficiency ended up there that I had to make the main-deck cards relatively simple. The little engine I'd been pleased with was using up the freedom I wanted elsewhere.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    It wasn't wasted work. It told me something important about the structure I was building. But I'd have learned the wrong lesson if I'd tried to rescue it by adding another layer on top.
- type: heading-2
  text: "Removing something can reveal what is actually working"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    A later playtest gave me a different version of the same lesson. I'd spent time thinking through Styles and Sequences for my card game. Both connected to the kind of expressive performance I wanted. I liked the ideas and could describe the role each was meant to play.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Then we tried playing without them. The core game still had a surprisingly good rhythm. There were still decisions, reactions and reasons to care what the other player did. I didn't miss the two systems as much as I'd expected.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    That doesn't prove Styles or Sequences are bad ideas, and I wouldn't claim a single test settles their future. What it did was give me a much better comparison. Instead of asking whether they sounded exciting, I could ask **what experience they added beyond what was already there**.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Sometimes the most useful thing about removing a mechanic is discovering that the game continues without it. Other times the missing piece becomes obvious immediately. Either result makes the design clearer.
- type: heading-2
  text: "I look for the relationship, not just the feature"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    This connects to the way I think about LEGO bricks. I don't find the most interesting design in a pile of individually impressive pieces. I find it in the places where they overlap and support each other.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    A movement ability can change how a level is read. A resource cost can change when that ability is worth using. An enemy's behaviour can make the same movement useful in a new way. Those connections can create depth without requiring three unrelated minigames.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    But connections can be messy too. If every action triggers a chain of extra procedures, the player may stop thinking about where to move or what to do and start thinking about which rules they haven't resolved yet. The mechanic is technically connected. It just isn't necessarily helping.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    When something feels crowded, I ask which decisions the player actually cares about. Can two systems serve the same goal? Does one duplicate the other? Is a new rule making an existing choice more interesting, or just giving us another step to remember?
- type: heading-2
  text: "How I decide what earns a place"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I try to start from the experience. If I want players to feel clever for anticipating each other, I need opportunities to read intentions and respond. If I want a sense of momentum, I need actions that lead naturally into what comes next. Those are useful goals, but they're not names of features.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Then I look at what each proposed mechanic contributes to them. I can build a rough version, remove one piece or deliberately exaggerate its effect. I can watch whether players change how they act, not just whether they can follow the rules.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    If a mechanic is doing valuable work, I want to keep that value. I might combine it with another system, make it optional, simplify the supporting procedure or save it for a different game. Removing the implementation doesn't have to mean giving up the intention.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I also try not to mistake familiarity for quality. After spending time designing something, I can explain its importance very convincingly. Testing it without the feature is one way to find out whether that importance exists for the player too.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    [BLOCK]When two mechanics seem good individually but make the game worse together, I don't think the answer is automatically to pick a winner. I want to understand what each one was meant to give the player, and whether their connection is creating something worth keeping.[/BLOCK]
