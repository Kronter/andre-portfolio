    One of the things I enjoy about making games is when a mechanic is doing exactly what you designed it to do, yet the result still feels wrong.

    That's where I've ended up with card draw in [VIOLET]**Tracers**[/VIOLET].

    Tracers is a card game about competing through movement, rhythm and improvisation. Players build a contest through alternating Maneuvers, generating Flow, Hype and Momentum as they go. I want a Jam to feel like two crews constantly reading and challenging one another, not two players taking isolated turns to assemble the biggest number.

    Extra cards seemed like an obvious way to support that. Give players more options, help them recover from awkward hands, keep the game flowing.

    And they did. Perhaps a little too well.

    [NOTE]***Quick note:*** This is part of my Prototype Two development work. The draw observations are from play; deck manipulation and the Auras below are directions I want to test, rather than finished solutions.[/NOTE]
- type: heading-2
  text: "When a Jam becomes a solo performance"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    A few playtest observations pushed me to look at draw much more carefully. It can be extremely powerful, even when its impact is contained to a single Jam.

    The problem isn't simply having a bigger hand. In Tracers, a card can represent several possibilities. You can play it as a Maneuver, but you can also discard it through Improv to generate resources based on its printed cost. Those resources help pay for other Maneuvers and can contribute to getting your Tracers into play.

    [BLOCK]So an additional card isn't just another option. It can be fuel, flexibility and another step in the same sequence.[/BLOCK]

    In the worst cases, that starts to create what I think of as *soloing*: one player keeps finding ways to continue while the other increasingly feels like they're watching. The rules still alternate turns, but the experience stops feeling like a contest.

    That matters more to me than whether the draw effect is mathematically efficient.
- type: heading-2
  text: "The actual problem isn't drawing cards"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I don't want to remove card draw. An expensive Maneuver that pays off with a fresh card can be satisfying. A Tracer ability that creates a brief draw opportunity can be interesting. Sometimes drawing is exactly what a design needs.

    What I'm questioning is how easily and frequently the game hands out *unconditional* cards.

    Drawing increases the cards I have available. Drawing and then discarding improves my options without necessarily increasing hand size. Looking at the top few cards and rearranging them changes what comes later. Returning a specific card from the discard pile to my hand can be even more powerful than a random draw.

    That last one matters. I've started treating recovery effects such as Recall, and even ways of replaying cards through Encore, with the same caution as draw. If extra cards are strong, getting back precisely the card I want deserves scrutiny too.

    For now, I'd rather reserve straightforward card advantage for a few special, expensive or meaningfully conditional effects. The rest of the game can do something more interesting.
- type: heading-2
  text: "Preparing a move instead of finding another move"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The direction I'm drawn to is deck manipulation.

    Other TCGs have explored ideas such as Scry, Surveil, Fateseal and Connive, along with plenty of variations. I'm interested in what makes those effects useful, not in importing their rules wholesale.

    Looking ahead, choosing what stays on top, sending cards to the discard pile, or improving a future hand creates a different kind of decision. Instead of asking, “How do I extend this Jam one more time?”, I can ask, “What am I setting up for the next one?”

    That feels closer to what I want Tracers to be about.

    It's not automatically safe. Sending a card to the discard pile can be a benefit if I can later replay or recover it. Manipulating an opponent's draws can become frustrating if they lose meaningful choices. The exact costs and conditions still matter.

    But there's more room to explore than simply adding another **Draw 1**.
- type: heading-2
  text: "Auras as a trace left behind"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    This brought me back to [VIOLET]**Auras**[/VIOLET].

    The version I want to test is simple: a Maneuver creates an Aura that stays visible on the play surface. It doesn't generate a recurring benefit every turn. It's single-use, and its first job is to help prepare the next Jam.

    Most of these would be preparation effects. Perhaps you inspect and arrange upcoming cards before refilling your hand. A smaller number might let you draw when you've met a condition.

    I like the thematic connection. You make a move now that leaves something behind. That changes how you approach the next part of the performance.

    There are practical questions. In Tracers, players normally refill their hands to eight at the end of a Jam. An effect that manipulates the top of the deck probably wants to happen before that refill. One that actually gives an extra card needs careful timing. [SIDENOTE]We haven't decided that part yet.[/SIDENOTE]

    [BLOCK]For now, the aim is simple: create the Aura through a Maneuver, use it once while preparing the next Jam, and remove it.[/BLOCK]

    Later I want to explore Auras that wait until a particular condition is met. They'd still be single-use; what changes is *when* they expire.
- type: heading-2
  text: "Why not play an Aura directly?"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    There are two directions worth considering.

    One is a standalone Aura: pay to put it into play, generate no resources, receive a benefit later. The other is a Maneuver that generates an Aura as part of doing something now.

    I'm starting with generated Auras because they let me test the idea without introducing another card type immediately. But standalone Auras aren't inherently wrong. Tracers already has Maneuvers that generate zero resources, so zero generation isn't a reason to dismiss them.

    A standalone Aura could create an interesting commitment: give something up now to improve your position later.

    It could also introduce complexity that this version of the game doesn't need.

    So for Prototype Two, generated Auras come first. The standalone version stays on the table as an option. I don't want to introduce a dedicated Aura area or a hard limit before testing gives me a reason.
- type: heading-2
  text: "What I want to learn next"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The next test isn't about proving Auras are better than draw. It's about seeing whether changing *when* players receive value improves the feel of a Jam.

    I'll compare a few existing draw and recovery effects with hand filtering, deck setup and conditional alternatives. I'll also test a small number of generated Auras, mostly preparation, with a few conditional draws. I want to see what happens to Improv, how often a player can sustain a Jam almost alone, and whether both players still have meaningful decisions.

    I'm not interested in making every possible version of every card just to fill a spreadsheet. A few carefully chosen comparisons should tell me more than a huge collection of untested variations.

    The design lesson I'm chasing is one I keep encountering with Tracers: more freedom isn't automatically more interaction. Sometimes giving one player more ways to keep going makes it harder for the other player to participate.

    [BLOCK]I'd rather have both players thinking about the next move than one player always finding another card to play.[/BLOCK]

    If deck manipulation and Auras help with that, they might do more than fix a balance problem. They might make Tracers feel more like the game I've been trying to build.
