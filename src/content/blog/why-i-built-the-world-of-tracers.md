- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The image I keep coming back to is two rival crews leaving a mark on the same city. They both want to win, but neither owns the whole performance.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    [NOTE]***Quick note:*** This is a worldbuilding direction, rather than a finished setting bible or a promise that every idea becomes a rule.[/NOTE]
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    But the result isn't a knocked-out opponent. It's a performance that neither crew could have created alone—and a new mark left on the city.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    That's the image I've been working toward with **Tracers**. It gave a card game about competing resources and alternating moves a world that could explain [VIOLET]*why*[/VIOLET] people would want to play that way.
- type: heading-2
  text: "I didn't want another game about hitting things"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The first motivation was closer to home than the worldbuilding itself. I wanted a competitive card game I could share with people who enjoy strategy but don't always enjoy direct confrontation. My partner is comfortable with complex games; what she tends to prefer is interaction that doesn't revolve around attacking someone else's pieces or repeatedly saying they can't play.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    That preference became a useful design challenge. Could I make a game feel competitive without relying on creatures fighting, health totals dropping or constant card cancellation?
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The answer wasn't to remove conflict. A competition without pressure can quickly become two people playing separate solitaire games. I still wanted players watching each other, adapting, committing, bluffing and occasionally getting in each other's way. I just wanted the confrontation to have a different shape.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I kept returning to the idea of a performance. Two dancers in a battle aren't merely ignoring each other, but they aren't necessarily trying to injure each other either. A wrestler can control a crowd as deliberately as they control an opponent. In capoeira, the relationship between performers can be simultaneously playful, competitive and cooperative. Those examples suggested a kind of rivalry I found more interesting for this project.
- type: heading-2
  text: "From a card game to an underground sport"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    That line of thinking became **Tracing**: an underground sport built around movement, style and improvisation. Crews move through their environment using elements of skating, parkour, dancing and acrobatics, enhanced by their own equipment and abilities. Their performances are called **Jams**, and their actions are **Maneuvers**.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I liked this framing because it made a competitive *exchange* the main event. If I play a Maneuver, I'm adding a move to the Jam. If my opponent responds, the performance changes. Neither of us has complete control over what it becomes.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    There's another part I particularly like: Tracing isn't only a contest. In the world concept, the movements leave marks—paint, light or other traces—so the two crews can produce a shared mural as they compete. A match can be a rivalry, a spectacle and an act of collaborative creation at the same time.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    This is a worldbuilding direction, not a rule saying players must literally draw a picture at the table. It gives me a visual and emotional target: the aftermath of a Jam should feel like the outcome of *both* players' choices, not a victory animation that belongs to one side alone.
- type: heading-2
  text: "Why fantasy, runes and near-future technology?"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I didn't want the setting to be ordinary modern street sport, but I also didn't want technology so futuristic that a flip, a wheel or a wall would stop feeling recognisable. The direction in my design notes sits somewhere between fantasy and a near-future city: neon, customized gear, arcane sparks and **magitech**—[SIDENOTE]technology powered or modified by magic and runes[/SIDENOTE].
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    That gives the performers permission to do spectacular things without making every trick feel arbitrary. A rune-powered skate or an unusual piece of movement equipment can suggest a particular style before anyone reads its rules. The fantasy side also opens the cast beyond humans: elves, goblins, dwarves, ogres and other people can approach the same sport with different silhouettes, techniques and personalities.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I was drawn to the kinetic energy of *Air Gear*, the theatricality of professional wrestling, hip-hop and breaking culture, and the kind of energetic city that could sit between contemporary streets and fantasy technology. Those are reference points, not a plan to reproduce their worlds.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    For the visual direction, I explored Shibuya-punk and superflat-pop influences, expressive posing and bold clothing. I want strong colour and striking shapes, but with a little more rendering and dimensionality than a purely flat treatment. Anime and manga have influenced the movement and energy, while a distinct card-game identity remains the aim.
- type: heading-2
  text: "The resource names began telling a story"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    As the systems developed, I had three important resources: **Flow, Hype and Momentum**. Their jobs in the rules need to be distinct and readable, but their names also offered a way to think about what happens inside a Jam.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    **Flow** is the continuity and control of a performance: settling into the movement, finding a rhythm, making an action look intentional. **Hype** is what catches the audience, the risky flourish or show-off moment that makes people react. **Momentum** is the force that carries the action forward, physically and competitively, even after a big move changes the pace.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Those meanings aren't strict physics. A spectacular trick could involve all three. The distinction is useful because a player choosing how to spend or generate resources can imagine a different kind of commitment, rather than just selecting one of three arbitrary currencies.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    At the end of a Jam, the game compares the competing resource totals. The contest is about how the performances stack up, not who lost more health. The triangular generation system adds a second layer: the type of Maneuver I choose and the resource it helps build aren't always the same. That lets a simple choice express preparation, rhythm and changing priorities.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The important test is whether players can understand those decisions at the table. The theme should illuminate the mechanics, not require them to learn a fictional sport before they can take a turn.
- type: heading-2
  text: "A crew gives the strategy a face"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The world also helped me think about the people doing the performing. In an earlier approach, a single Featured Tracer rotated according to match outcomes. Later, I moved toward three performers assigned to Flow, Hype and Momentum, each working toward an **Entrance** as the player builds the relevant resource.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I find that more evocative than simply buying a character from a menu. A new performer arriving can feel like a member of the crew recognizing the moment to join the show. It gives resource management a visible moment of progression and gives each Tracer the potential to feel like a particular athlete or personality.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The details still have to earn their place mechanically. Entrance thresholds, ability timing and resource acceleration have been subjects of further design and validation planning. I'm not treating the world fiction as proof that a specific implementation is balanced.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The same applies to **Styles**. I like the idea of a crew having a recognizable identity—something between a dance genre, a fighting-game stance and a wrestler's signature finish. Styles and card Sequences offered expressive possibilities, but later minimal playtests didn't miss them, so they're outside the streamlined core for now. The fantasy survives even if a particular subsystem doesn't.
- type: heading-2
  text: "A world is useful when it changes my decisions"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I'm still exploring the details of the city, its territories, crew culture and underground leagues. Some league names and wider lore ideas were brainstormed rather than finalized. I don't want to pretend the world has a complete historical timeline or an official canon when much of that work is still open.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    What I *do* have is a clear direction against which to test new ideas. Does this card suggest a recognizable move or performance? Does it make the opposing crew worth watching? Can a character's identity come through without adding another layer of bookkeeping? Does the art communicate movement rather than a static collection of special effects?
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The best part of Tracing as a setting is that **competition and cooperation can exist in the same moment**. Players are trying to outstyle each other, but they're building a single event together. That tension is the reason I chose this world in the first place.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    [BLOCK]It's also why I'm happy to keep changing its rules. If a mechanic makes the experience less expressive or less readable, the world gives me a reason to question the mechanic—not an excuse to keep it.[/BLOCK]
