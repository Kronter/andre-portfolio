    The bit I wanted to capture was the moment somebody new joins a crew's performance. Ideally you can see them getting ready and think, ‘Oh, if she gets one more Flow, that Tracer's coming in.’
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    [NOTE]***Quick note:*** The timing below describes the later prototype direction. The balance comparisons remain planned work.[/NOTE]
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    It took me a few goes to get from rotating a **Featured Tracer** to the [VIOLET]Entrance[/VIOLET] system I'm testing now.
- type: heading-2
  text: "The problem with the Featured approach"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    An earlier version made one of a player's three Tracers Featured at a time. The system explored rotating the active Tracer, including rules tied to losing a Jam. At one point, a particularly harsh version could penalise a player who had no valid Tracer available. I could explain the intention: keep characters moving through the performance and prevent a single dominant choice.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    But the procedure was carrying too much weight. Players needed to track which character was Featured, what happened after a loss and which automatic changes were required. The dramatic fantasy was being mediated by administration.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The question I needed to ask was not 'How do I make the rotation system more detailed?' It was 'What should it feel like when a Tracer becomes part of the Jam?' That reframing opened a different approach.
- type: heading-2
  text: "From forced rotation to earned arrival"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The new direction placed three Tracers outside the Maneuver deck, each assigned to Flow, Hype or Momentum [SIDENOTE]after the player saw their final opening hand[/SIDENOTE]. Each Tracer would have a printed Entrance requirement tied to the resource it was assigned. Reach the requirement and the Tracer enters, eventually remaining in play across Jams.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I liked the link because it attached progression to the main contest. The decision to build Flow was no longer only about scoring Flow at Jam end. It could also bring a character closer to their Entrance. What looks like one action now carries both an immediate competitive goal and a medium-term progression goal.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The old Featured rotation was replaced for current testing. That doesn't mean the previous experiment was useless. It helped identify the emotional job—dramatic entrances and character identity—while showing me one way of implementing it that created too much friction.
- type: heading-2
  text: "Printed requirements make characters different"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I initially considered a common Entrance threshold. It would have been easy to explain: every Tracer arrives at the same number. But a universal number ignores that Tracers may have very different power and strategic roles. A modest ability and a major payoff should not necessarily arrive at the same pace.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The design moved toward individual printed requirements. This created room for different timing curves. It also meant I had to think about how the resource triangle, card generation and Improv could accelerate or delay each character.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    A threshold is not just a cost. It determines how often a player sees a character contribute, what goals compete with bringing that character out, and whether the arrival feels like an achievement or a formality.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I also had to be careful with what the characters did on arrival. A Tracer entering with an activated ability immediately usable could create a very different tempo spike from one whose ability becomes available on a later turn.
- type: heading-2
  text: "Did I have enough Flow when my turn ended?"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    **When do I check?** At the end of my turn, after the effects have resolved, I look at how much of the assigned resource I still have. If I reached the requirement and then spent some of it, my Tracer might have to wait.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    A passing turn still gets its ordinary Entrance Check. There is no additional check later in the same Jam simply because an opponent grants that player more resources. Tracers enter ready by default, but their activated abilities first become usable on a later own turn; printed card exceptions may specify otherwise.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    These details might sound like rulebook housekeeping. To me, they are essential to a trustworthy player experience. They tell players what they can plan toward and when they should expect a payoff. A surprise timing interpretation is not a fun strategic surprise.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    They also keep the game physically manageable. Players can resolve a turn, inspect the visible resource state and perform a predictable check. A rule that relies on remembering every temporary threshold crossed throughout the turn would be harder to operate.
- type: heading-2
  text: "Improv complicated the arrival curve"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The same conversion mechanic that made hands flexible also made Entrances easier to accelerate. When Improv changed from always granting one resource to granting the printed cost symbols, a discard could push a resource much further. Combined with card draw, it sometimes enabled several characters to enter faster than expected.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I don't view that observation as automatic evidence that Improv should be capped or that every Tracer needs a higher threshold. The more useful question is what rapid Entrances do to the match. Do players still have counterplay? Are decisions still interesting? Does the game reward planning, or does the first big draw simply decide the outcome?
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    For that reason, my later validation plan separated mathematical or simulated checks from physical playtesting, and compared character-specific tuning against changes to Maneuver economy. Those experiments were planned; they weren't finished balance results.
- type: heading-2
  text: "Progression has to change the present"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    An Entrance feels exciting only if the new character changes the player's choices or reinforces the performance. If it merely adds another reminder, it may not be doing enough. If it wins the Jam without a meaningful decision, it may be doing too much.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I like testing this by watching the turns immediately before and after an Entrance. Was the player deliberately working toward it? Did they sacrifice an alternative for the arrival? Once the Tracer arrived, did their next choice change in a way they could explain?
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Those observations would tell me whether the progression system genuinely belongs in the core loop. It would also tell me more than merely measuring the average turn on which a Tracer appears.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    [BLOCK]What I want to see is the other player noticing who's about to join my crew. If they can see the Entrance coming, the turn *before* the character arrives can be part of the fun too.[/BLOCK]
