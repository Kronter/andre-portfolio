- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Give me three ways across a river and I'll probably want to try all three. Ask me which one I'd choose the second time, though, and I'd like a better reason than ‘I haven't seen the raft animation yet.’
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    [NOTE]***Quick note:*** More options can be useful. I am interested in what makes those options worth choosing between.[/NOTE]
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    When I look at a choice, that's what I'm trying to get at. **What would make me pick differently next time?**
- type: heading-2
  text: "The bridge, the swim and the raft"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Take a bridge, a swim and a raft. If all three get me to the same bank in the same time with the same things in my pockets, I can pick the one I like the look of. There's very little to plan around, though.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    That's not necessarily bad [SIDENOTE]expressive choices can matter[/SIDENOTE]. Choosing how a character behaves is itself a kind of play. But if the design promises strategic choice, those routes need distinct consequences. Maybe swimming is fast but requires abandoning equipment. Maybe the bridge is safe but guarded. Maybe a raft takes preparation while preserving supplies.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Now the player is weighing **time, risk and future opportunity**. The same three options have become a more interesting system without adding a fourth route.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I find it useful to distinguish the *number of actions* from the *number of meaningful tradeoffs*. A smaller action set can support considerable depth if the environment changes their relative value.
- type: heading-2
  text: "Situations make familiar actions interesting"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    A jump button is simple. The experience it creates depends on where and when the player uses it. A jump across a small gap is a movement check. The same jump during a chase with shifting platforms can become a question of timing, risk and confidence.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I admire the way games associated with Shigeru Miyamoto often make familiar interactions worth exploring through context and immediate response. I don't think there is a single Miyamoto design formula to copy, and I'm not claiming this is his terminology. What resonates with me is the emphasis on what happens when players act and how the world reacts.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    That is why I often want to test an interaction before designing a large surrounding structure. Does it feel responsive? Can the player anticipate the result? Can they discover a use for it that the instructions did not explicitly spell out? Those questions reveal whether the action can carry enough interest to justify deeper systems.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Adding abilities before answering them can create a long list of buttons without a coherent experience.
- type: heading-2
  text: "Tradeoffs are not just penalties"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    When I say a choice needs consequences, I don't mean everything must have a punishment. A tradeoff can be about identity, pacing, information, preparation or personal expression. Perhaps choosing a slow path lets a player discover more about the world. Perhaps a fast route lets them arrive before an event changes.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Suppose the raft carries extra supplies. That's a lovely advantage on the way to an expedition and fairly useless if I'm heading home with an empty bag. I like that the very same option can be worth a second look on the return trip.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I like asking whether two different players could reasonably make different decisions from the same state. If so, what are they valuing differently? If I can explain those priorities, I have a stronger basis for designing and balancing the alternatives.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    There is no requirement for perfect balance between every option. Sometimes a specialised tool is deliberately best in a specialised situation. The challenge is giving that situation a reason to occur and making the relationship understandable.
- type: heading-2
  text: "Information determines what can be chosen"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    A decision can exist mathematically while being invisible to the player. Imagine a crafting game where soil quality affects plant growth, but no part of the interface indicates which soil a plant prefers. Players may technically have a choice between several planting locations, yet most can't reason about it without trial and error or external reference material.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Discovery can be fun; opaque bookkeeping often isn't. I try to separate uncertainty about *what might happen* from uncertainty about *how the rules work*. The first can create curiosity and tension. The second can prevent players from forming a plan at all.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    A little feedback can transform the experience: soil appearance, plant behaviour, a change in growth animation, or a short in-world clue. Players can begin making informed predictions. They don't need the entire formula revealed, but they need enough signal to connect action and consequence.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    This also means that accessibility isn't necessarily a matter of reducing options. Making their implications visible can allow more players to engage with the same depth.
- type: heading-2
  text: "The real cost of adding an option"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Each new action asks for animation, control mapping, feedback, teaching, balancing and testing. It also changes the value of every existing action. A new traversal ability may erase a previously meaningful route choice. A new healing item may undermine the pressure that makes exploration compelling.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I try not to evaluate a feature only by asking whether it is fun alone. I ask what it does to the choices surrounding it. Does it open a new style of play? Does it collapse an existing decision? Can it be understood at the moment players need it?
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Sometimes the best feature is a small change to an old interaction rather than a new mechanic. A contextual use for a familiar tool can create a surprising decision while preserving a clear control vocabulary.
- type: heading-2
  text: "A practical test I use"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    For a prototype, I like to write down a decision in plain language: 'The player must choose whether to take the safe route or spend a limited resource to save time.' Then I watch what happens. Do players recognise both alternatives? Does the decision change when their circumstances change? Can they explain why they chose one?
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    If every tester chooses the same option for the same reason, I look for a missing tradeoff or an irrelevant alternative. If they choose differently but cannot understand the consequences, I look at feedback and communication. If different choices produce interesting outcomes, I have a strong basis for further iteration.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    [BLOCK]I'd go back to the river with the same three routes and change what the player is carrying. If they stop to reconsider the crossing, those three options are already doing quite a bit of work.[/BLOCK]
