- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    We tried a game of **Tracers** without Styles or Sequences. I liked both mechanics and had spent a fair bit of time working out how they should fit. What surprised me was how little I missed them while we were playing.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    [NOTE]***Quick note:*** These are observations from the versions described below, rather than a claim that the current rules are finished.[/NOTE]
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Since showing the game in my NZGDC talk, I've had a chance to try it with fewer moving parts. Sequences were one of the first things I wanted another look at.
- type: heading-2
  text: "A Sequence that exists mostly in your memory"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    One early idea involved building Sequences from cards played in order. The concept made thematic sense: a performance should be about how moves connect. But competitive play exposed a practical problem. Players weren't always sure where a Sequence began, whether it had ended or what had reset.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The game understood the Sequence in the designer's imagination. The table didn't show it reliably.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    One line of revision was to declare Sequences explicitly and group the relevant cards so the boundary became visible. That improved the communication problem, [SIDENOTE]at least conceptually[/SIDENOTE]. But it also raised another question: what did the mechanic add to the experience [VIOLET]*right now*[/VIOLET]?
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    When we eventually played without either system, we could still read each other's moves and compete over the Jam. That was the bit I'd been worried about losing.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    So I moved them out of the minimal prototype and kept them as possible future depth rather than mandatory starting rules.
- type: heading-2
  text: "When useful flexibility becomes acceleration"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Another development involved **Improv**, the option to discard a card for resources instead of using it normally. I liked the pressure it created: the cards in your hand weren't only potential moves; they were also fuel.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    An earlier one-resource version evolved into a printed-cost-based version. Now the card's cost affected how much it could contribute when discarded. That relationship made card evaluation richer, but it also interacted with another system: the Tracers' resource-linked Entrances.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Playtests indicated that cost-based Improv could accelerate several Entrances. More draw also became extra resource fuel, which could make an advantage feed itself. What looked like useful flexibility at the level of one card was also an amplifying loop across the whole economy.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The response wasn't simply to put a blanket cap on everything. It was to identify which part of the loop created the problem, then compare focused adjustments. Entrance timing and Improv timing were clarified; other numerical and card-text questions remain open.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    A good system relationship can create a bad balance relationship. It's worth celebrating the first without ignoring the second.
- type: heading-2
  text: "Simple scoring can still produce a slow game"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    At the end of a Jam, the documented game compares Flow, Hype and Momentum. Tied totals award no Points. On paper, that's easy to explain and seems fair. In play, ties could make the race drag.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I considered ways to give the Lead an advantage in tie-breaking. But one proposed exception—allowing the Lead to break just one tie each Jam—introduced its own little rule to remember. I rejected that option. A fix to a pacing problem shouldn't create an equally irritating tracking problem.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Other scoring alternatives are still worth comparing. The important thing is to observe the actual number of ties, how long Jams take and whether players feel they had meaningful options. The fact that a proposed rule sounds elegant doesn't tell me whether it solves the play problem.
- type: heading-2
  text: "Interaction needs a decision, not just information"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The alternating Maneuver structure improved the cadence, but later play still revealed moments of parallel solitaire. Players could see the opponent's actions and yet not have enough reason to react to them.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I experimented with the idea of effects that ask you to read or predict the opponent's next resource choice. If they play Flow next, perhaps your earlier commitment pays off. The possibility is appealing because it encourages attention without demanding a dense interrupt stack.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    What I'd like to watch for in a prediction test is quite simple. Does the other player look at my prediction, look back at their hand, and reconsider what they were going to play?
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Similarly, letting someone glimpse an opponent's hand is not automatically interaction. If the information doesn't influence a later choice, it may simply be trivia.
- type: heading-2
  text: "Wording and layout are rules"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    A few issues were less glamorous but just as important. Who chooses a resource when an effect changes it? Is Improv allowed on the opponent's turn? Does a Tracer enter immediately or after effects resolve? A phrase that makes sense when I explain it aloud can be ambiguous when two other people sit down to play.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    In the streamlined direction, Improv is limited to the active player's turn before the Maneuver that ends it, and Entrance checking happens after that turn's effects resolve. Those are examples of timing decisions becoming explicit so that players can reason about them.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I've also learned to question card terminology. A familiar keyword can save reading, but only when players interpret it in the intended way. Something short can be less clear than a slightly longer, ordinary phrase. The table is where that becomes obvious.
- type: heading-2
  text: "What I'd bring back"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I still want Tracers to feel like a competitive performance with strategic expression and indirect pressure. Removing a Sequence from the minimal test doesn't abandon that goal. Revising Improv doesn't betray the resource system. Changing an Entrance's timing doesn't change why an Entrance should feel exciting.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    [BLOCK]I'd happily try Sequences again if I find a move that's enjoyable to put together and easy for the other player to follow. For now, I quite like being able to sit down and play without explaining them.[/BLOCK]
