- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    If I wanted to practise designing clearer digital games, a fairly odd place to start would be to take away my screen. That was part of the attraction of making a physical card game. I work on digital games. I know how handy it is to have a button light up at exactly the moment a move becomes legal. What would I do for the person across the table when I couldn’t light anything up?
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    For a personal project I can start with the genre, the setting, even a mechanic I've wanted an excuse to try. For a learning project I try to turn that around.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    A better question is: **what do I want to get better at, and what could I make that would force me to practise it?**
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    That shift became the centre of my NZGDC talk, [VIOLET]*What Physical Games Taught Me About Better Game Design*[/VIOLET]. Rather than treating a personal project only as a possible product, I began treating it as a training environment.
- type: heading-2
  text: "Learning is more than collecting explanations"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Books, talks, courses and conversations with other designers are valuable [SIDENOTE]I use them too[/SIDENOTE]. But I don't always understand something properly until I try to apply it, discover why my first interpretation was inadequate and adjust.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I can explain why a player needs feedback and still write a perfectly sensible rule that only works because I’m sitting there explaining it. The awkward bit about playing my own paper prototype is that I know whose turn it is, what just happened and where the next card goes. It takes effort to notice how much of the interface is currently me.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Repeated cycles of doing, failing, reflecting and adjusting help turn knowledge into a habit. Different people learn differently, and a good learning project gives you practical problems you can't solve by simply repeating a definition.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    That makes constraints surprisingly useful. A constraint takes away familiar tools and reveals which parts of your understanding were being supported by them.
- type: heading-2
  text: "Find the crutch and remove it"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    As someone who works on digital games, I can rely on the engine to do a great deal: enforce rules, track state, calculate outcomes, organise information, display tooltips and guide players through actions.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Those are strengths of the medium. But they can also hide poor design communication. A feature might technically function because the UI prevents illegal actions, while the player still has no real idea why something happened.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    One way I challenged myself was by working in a physical medium to remove those conveniences. There would be no invisible rule enforcement. No automatic counter updates. No interface quietly handling timing. If players needed a piece of information, I had to put it somewhere they could find it or explain how they could derive it.
- type: heading-2
  text: "Choose a project that produces the right problems"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    A physical learning project can be useful when I want more practice with interconnected systems, onboarding, clarity and player interaction. Making rules tangible forces me to decide where information lives, how players recognize a valid action and how much they must remember between decisions.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    But that isn't the right training project for every skill. If I wanted to practise onboarding, I might make a tiny print-and-play game and hand it to people without an explanation. If I wanted to practise narrative agency, a short solo RPG or live-action scenario might force me to account for choices that don't fit a dialogue tree. If I wanted to practise spatial design, a physical puzzle room or tabletop space could expose sightlines and affordances in new ways.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The choice of medium is itself part of the learning design. The useful project is not necessarily the one closest to your day job; it may be the one that makes your comfortable assumptions least reliable.
- type: heading-2
  text: "How I set up a learning project"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The exercise I use starts with a **skill** and a **crutch**. I pick something I'd like to practise, then take away one tool that usually does part of it for me. I build just enough to run into the difficulty, try it with someone, and write down what actually happened. The last bit is taking one of those observations back to my normal work.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The reflection matters because I could easily walk away from that session thinking I need a better explanation. A different diagnosis would lead me to move two cards closer together. In the next game I can leave them there, let someone else take the turn and see which questions still come up.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Likewise, a prototype that goes smoothly doesn't automatically prove my design is strong. It may mean the test was too comfortable, the participants shared my assumptions, or the question was too broad to reveal anything useful.
- type: heading-2
  text: "Transfer the lesson, not the mechanic"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Working outside my usual medium has taught me to notice invisible state, crowded systems and weak goal structure. None of those lessons requires me to recreate the original exercise inside a digital feature.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I can apply them by making a UI state easier to read, shortening the delay between an action and its consequence, or ensuring players have something meaningful to work toward right now as well as later. The useful transfer is a sharper way of identifying problems, not a mandate to copy the tabletop solution.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    That difference followed me back to work. On a digital event I was designing, I recreated part of the feature physically. I’d had recent practice putting a decision in front of someone before building all the things around it. I was more willing to rethink an early version because I could already see which part of the player’s decision I wanted to keep.
- type: heading-2
  text: "A project is allowed to succeed as a lesson"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    One of the freedoms of this approach is that the project doesn't have to ship. I still want to make something worthwhile for players. I still care about quality. But its value isn't exhausted by whether it becomes a commercial release.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    A learning exercise can keep changing as each version presents a narrower question. I might remove a favourite feature from a minimal test, or discover that two good ideas don't connect well. Those results remain useful beyond the individual exercise because they teach me what to look for next time, even when the most successful outcome is a smaller design.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    [BLOCK]If I wanted to practise explaining game state tomorrow, I'd put a few cards on a table and give someone a turn. The interesting bit for me would be the first question they have to ask.[/BLOCK]
