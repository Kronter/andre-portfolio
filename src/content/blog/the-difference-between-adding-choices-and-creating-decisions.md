- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    It's fairly easy to give a player more things they can do. I can add another button, a new card effect, an upgrade branch or a second way to complete an objective. The design document gets bigger, and the player has more options.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    But I've learned not to mistake that for giving them more interesting decisions. Sometimes the extra option is clearly the strongest choice. Sometimes the options do the same job in slightly different ways. Sometimes a player has to navigate more menus without having a new reason to think.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    When I talk about meaningful choices in game design, this is the distinction I'm trying to get at. [VIOLET]**A choice becomes interesting when its alternatives matter to each other, and the consequence matters to the player.**[/VIOLET]
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    [NOTE]***Quick note:*** I'm not suggesting every action needs to be a difficult strategic decision. Games need rhythm, expression and moments where doing something simply feels good. I'm interested in the places where we deliberately ask a player to choose.[/NOTE]
- type: heading-2
  text: "More options don't always mean more decisions"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Imagine I've made three cards for the same role. One costs less, produces more and has no drawback. Unless there's some special interaction I'm missing, I haven't made a three-way decision. I've made one useful card and two disappointing ones.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I can solve that by making the numbers closer, but that isn't the only answer. More interestingly, I might give the cards different strengths. One helps me immediately. One prepares something stronger for later. One is less efficient but gives me the resource I need for a different plan.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Now I have to look at the situation. There isn't necessarily a correct card independent of what I'm trying to achieve. The options have begun competing in a way the player can understand.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I don't need to make every option equal in every circumstance. In fact, that can make them feel interchangeable. I'd rather have choices that are good for different reasons, provided players have enough information to recognise those reasons.
- type: heading-2
  text: "A decision needs something worth giving up"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Opportunity cost is one of the most useful ways I think about this. If I spend a resource on one action, what else could I have done with it? If I take the safer route, what reward or information might I miss? If I use my strongest move now, what happens when I need it later?
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    A resource matters because gaining or spending it changes what I can do next. A card's cost matters because the same resources could pay for something else worthwhile. Turn timing matters because choosing to act now changes what the opponent can respond to later.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Those relationships do more than increase the number of legal moves. They create **more meaningful and interesting choices**. The player has a reason to hesitate, commit and sometimes regret not taking the alternative.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I like that last part, provided the consequence is fair. Regret can mean 'I knew the risk and chose badly.' That's different from 'I couldn't have known the rule worked that way.' One teaches the player about their decisions. The other teaches them to distrust the game.
- type: heading-2
  text: "The other player can make the same action mean something else"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I tend to enjoy games where the person across from me has a reason to pay attention while I'm taking my turn. That doesn't require every move to attack them directly.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Suppose I'm ahead in one part of a shared competition but behind in another. I can strengthen my lead, attempt to catch up elsewhere or set up a future move. What makes those choices interesting is partly what the other player has already done—and partly what I think they'll do next.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    If they respond by chasing the same objective, my original decision has a different consequence than if they concede it and focus somewhere else. The actions can remain mechanically simple while the decisions become richer because they're happening inside a relationship.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    This is why I pay attention to turn structure. A long uninterrupted turn can offer plenty of choices to the active player while giving the other person little to react to. Alternating smaller actions may make each choice less elaborate on its own but give both players more reasons to revise their plans.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Neither structure is inherently right. The point is that the [VIOLET]**timing of a choice changes what the choice means**[/VIOLET]. If I'm designing for a back-and-forth feeling, I need to support that through the rules, not just the fiction.
- type: heading-2
  text: "A player needs to see what the decision changes"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I can write an intricate set of consequences that would make a decision fascinating to a designer who knows the rules. That doesn't mean the person playing can see them.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    If a choice depends on a future resource, a cooldown or the order of previous actions, the player needs access to enough of that state to judge it. Not every outcome must be certain. Uncertainty can be exciting. But the player should know what they're risking and what kind of result they're trying to create.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    This is where systems design and UX meet for me. Moving information to the right place can create a better decision without changing the underlying maths at all. A visible cost, a clear timing window or a better name can turn a guess into a considered choice.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    It can work the other way too. If I keep polishing the presentation and players still don't have a reason to prefer one option over another, the problem might not be clarity. I might simply not have an interesting decision yet.
- type: heading-2
  text: "I would rather make a small set of actions richer"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I often come back to the idea of reusable building blocks. A mechanic doesn't have to gain three new modes to become deeper. Its meaning can change through the other systems it touches.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Movement is a simple example. Walking and jumping can be enough to produce a surprising number of decisions when level layout, timing, danger, rewards and information change around them. A single combat action can become an interesting commitment when an enemy telegraphs its response and the player has to decide whether to interrupt or reposition.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The same thinking applies to economies and progression. An upgrade can be meaningful because it changes how an existing ability works with the environment. A resource decision can matter because it affects both what I do now and what I can do afterwards. The useful depth lives in the overlaps.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Of course, I sometimes do need another tool or action. The goal isn't to make a game small for its own sake. It's to understand what new decision that addition creates, and whether a player would actually notice losing it.
- type: heading-2
  text: "What I watch when people play"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I don't think the best sign of a meaningful decision is necessarily how long someone stares at the screen. A player might act quickly because they've learned the situation well. Another might pause because they're confused.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I look for changes in intention. Does someone explain why they're saving a card? Do they change their plan after seeing an opponent's move? Can they point to what they gave up? Would the same player choose differently if the circumstances changed?
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Those questions help me tell the difference between adding variety and creating decisions. If there's one obvious answer no matter what happens around it, the alternatives probably need more work. If the player understands several good possibilities and has to commit to one, I'm much closer to what I wanted.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    [BLOCK]I don't measure the quality of a mechanic by how many options it adds. I look for the moment when two useful possibilities pull the player in different directions—and they can explain why choosing one means giving up the other.[/BLOCK]
