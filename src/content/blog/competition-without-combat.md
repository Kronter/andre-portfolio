- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I wanted a competitive card game that could be played with people who enjoy strategy but don't necessarily enjoy destroying the other player's plans. That isn't the same as wanting a game without tension. In fact, it creates a more specific design problem: how do two players genuinely compete when the central fantasy isn't attacking, eliminating or cancelling each other's cards?
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    This was one of the defining problems behind Tracers. The game became a contest of performances, where players build Flow, Hype and Momentum during a Jam. But a shared scoreboard doesn't automatically create interaction. Two people can race for points while barely noticing what the other person does.
- type: heading-2
  text: "Interaction isn't the same as interference"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    When I think about interaction, I separate *being affected by an opponent* from *having to make a decision because of an opponent*. The first can be as simple as someone taking points off you. The second is more interesting to me: their move changes what you think your best move is.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Direct attacks are one way to achieve that, but not the only one. A visible commitment, an opportunity the other player might claim, a changing score or a predicted action can all create pressure. A move can be interactive without saying, 'No, you can't do that.'
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I also don't want to pretend indirect interaction is automatically gentler. A hidden denial mechanic can be more frustrating than a clear attack. What matters is whether the player can understand the pressure, anticipate it and decide how to respond. A meaningful contest needs room for both agency and consequences.
- type: heading-2
  text: "Why alternating actions mattered"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The early turn structure made it too easy for one player to perform a long sequence while the other watched. I looked at alternating activation systems in tabletop wargames because they offer a useful rhythm: I do something, you do something, and each action changes the context for the next.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Translating that idea to a card game was not a direct copy. Wargame activations often involve units, positions and tactical threats. In my prototype, they became alternating Maneuver turns within a Jam. The point was the cadence, not the miniatures.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    That cadence makes more information relevant. If I play a Maneuver that increases Flow, you can respond before I play another Maneuver. If you push Hype, I can reconsider whether I want to chase it or invest somewhere else. The opponent's last decision becomes part of my next one.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    However, alternating turns only creates *opportunities* for interaction. It doesn't guarantee interesting ones. If all I do is add to my chosen resource while you add to yours, we may still be playing parallel games in smaller increments.
- type: heading-2
  text: "A scoring system can create pressure"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Each Jam compares the players' Flow, Hype and Momentum. Contested resources are worth points; the first to reach five points while uniquely ahead wins the match under the later accepted design. That creates several possible contests at once instead of a single damage race.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I like having a small number of visible fronts. When you see me committing to one resource, you have a choice: contest it, concentrate on another or influence the values through a card effect. Ideally, none of those is automatically best. The table should make the stakes legible without needing a verbal recap after every turn.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Ties taught me another lesson. An earlier rule awarded no points for tied resources, which could extend matches. Later I moved to a cleaner positive-tie rule in which the Jam's Lead wins a positive tied resource; a zero-to-zero tie still awards nothing. It is a specific balance and pacing choice, not a universal recommendation for competitive games.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Even a tiny tie rule affects how much players care about following an opponent's resource commitment. That makes it part of interaction design, not just scoring arithmetic.
- type: heading-2
  text: "Predictions create a conversation"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    One of the later playtest findings was that the game could still feel too much like parallel solitaire. Merely letting someone see a card in the opponent's hand wasn't enough if they couldn't use that information to make a meaningful choice.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    An avenue I began exploring was card-based prediction: a Maneuver can publicly establish a condition about the opponent's *next* played Flow, Hype or Momentum Maneuver. If the predicted type is played next, the card's conditional benefit resolves. In the later agreed timing, the opposing Maneuver resolves normally first, and then the prediction payoff happens. Unused predictions expire at the end of the Jam.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The aim is not to add an unlimited reaction action. It is to make the next ordinary turn a conversation. Your card says something about what you think I will do; I can see that condition and decide whether the expected response is worth triggering. Sometimes I might deliberately choose a different route. Sometimes I might accept the payoff because my own plan matters more.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    That is a richer interaction than a surprise cancellation precisely because the player can understand the choice before making it.
- type: heading-2
  text: "What remains experimental"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The prediction framework does not mean the actual cards are fully settled. Potential payoff families include resources, draws, Prep and opponent penalties. Their exact magnitudes and the fair cost of those effects remained under active design review in October 2026. I don't want to turn a promising direction into a claim that it has already been balanced through testing.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I would test whether a prediction changes the opponent's decision, not merely whether it triggers. If a player always ignores it, the condition may have little practical force. If they always avoid it, it may be functioning as a disguised prohibition. The interesting space is where players sometimes choose to trigger it and sometimes change plans, for reasons they can articulate.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    For me, competition without combat isn't about removing conflict. It's about relocating conflict into choices: what to reveal, where to commit, what to contest and when to let an opponent benefit. The performance theme gave those decisions a personality. The rules still have to make the other performer matter.
