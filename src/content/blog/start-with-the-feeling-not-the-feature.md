- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Here’s a small design problem I like. Imagine I’ve watered a neglected garden and come back tomorrow. What would make me stop at one particular plant before I open the list of things to do? If I can picture that moment, I’ve got somewhere to start.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The bit I'd want to keep coming back for is noticing a small change and working out what caused it. That gives me something to look for in each feature I add.
- type: heading-2
  text: "Features describe what we build"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    **Watering a plant** sounds like a perfectly sensible feature. If all it does is reset a timer, though, I might spend most of my time in the garden clearing a list of chores.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Now start with an [VIOLET]experience goal[/VIOLET]: 'I want players to feel that careful observation and small acts of care make a place come alive.' The feature list takes on a different meaning. Watering could be a decision about when to intervene, not a mandatory timer. Compost could influence which plants thrive together. New growth could reveal unexpected relationships between neighbouring flowers.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The mechanics are still familiar. What changes is the standard we use to judge them. A feature earns its place by contributing to the experience rather than merely satisfying a checklist.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    This doesn't mean I write down one emotion and treat it as an inflexible law. Players interpret games differently. A goal is a hypothesis about what the interaction should invite, and I have to check whether real play supports it.
- type: heading-2
  text: "What would a curious player do?"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    'Players should feel curious' isn't a test result. I need to ask what curiosity would look like. Would they investigate a strange object without being prompted? Try a second approach after their first attempt? Ask what might happen if they returned later?
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    For a sense of mastery, I might look for players predicting the result of an action and changing their approach deliberately. For a sense of belonging, I might look at whether they recognise characters, voluntarily revisit places, or invent small routines.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    In this imagined garden, counting return visits would tell me very little on its own. Someone who comes back to inspect a new shoot and someone who comes back because a timer is about to expire can look identical in the numbers. I’d want to hear what each person hoped to find when they opened the gate.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I also like asking players open questions after they have played. What did you want to do next? Which moment surprised you? When did you stop feeling in control? Their descriptions may reveal a different experience from the one I thought I designed.
- type: heading-2
  text: "Feelings emerge from relationships"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    A sense of discovery rarely comes from a single 'discover' button. It grows from environmental clues, uncertainty, feedback and the consequences of exploration. A sense of tension might emerge from time pressure, incomplete information and the cost of retreating.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    This is where my LEGO-brick view of design becomes useful. I can take familiar systems and ask what happens when their relationships reinforce one intended experience. Weather, exploration and preparation can create anticipation if the forecast changes how players plan. They can also create irritation if the forecast only forces repetitive maintenance.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The same pieces can create completely different emotions depending on timing, feedback and how much control the player has. That is why I don't want to design a system in isolation and assume the intended experience will arrive automatically.
- type: heading-2
  text: "The moment-to-moment interaction matters"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    A movement mechanic may be enjoyable simply because turning, accelerating and stopping feel responsive. A toy-like interaction might be satisfying before it has a score or a progression bar. If that foundation works, the environment can present increasingly interesting situations without needing to invent a completely new interaction each time.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Conversely, a beautiful progression structure cannot entirely rescue an action the player dreads repeating. If gathering is the main activity, I want the act of gathering to be worth some attention. If talking to characters is central, I want the rhythm of that exchange to feel appropriate.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I don't take this as a rule that every individual action must be endlessly entertaining. Some actions intentionally create quiet, contrast or preparation. The point is to understand their role in the overall experience rather than relying on rewards alone to make them tolerable.
- type: heading-2
  text: "Different time scales, one direction"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Working on a digital event made me think more carefully about how a goal looks at different distances. A player can know exactly what the next button does and still have no idea why they’d come back tomorrow. I’d carry that question into the garden too.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I'd like watering one plant to give me a little evidence about what that bed needs. Then tending the bed brings the larger garden along with it. I have a reason to care about today's action even if the finished garden is a long way off.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    A design can also fail when these horizons contradict one another. If the world invites relaxed experimentation but a harsh timer punishes curiosity, the systems are sending mixed signals. That might be deliberate in a survival experience; it might be a mistake in a cosy one.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I want to know which it is before making the timer more sophisticated.
- type: heading-2
  text: "Keep the goal while changing the solution"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Experience goals give me a way to discard mechanics without losing direction. If a crafting menu isn't creating satisfying preparation, I can try a different way to make preparation meaningful. If a quest marker overwhelms discovery, I can change how information reaches the player.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    That flexibility is particularly important during prototyping. I am not trying to prove that the first implementation was clever. I am trying to find a version that creates the experience I was looking for.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    [BLOCK]I’d try the smallest scene first. One bed, two visits and a change the player can notice on the second visit. Before I add another plant, I want to know what they think happened to the first one.[/BLOCK]
