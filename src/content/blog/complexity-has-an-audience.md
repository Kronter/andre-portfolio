- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I like complicated games. I enjoy learning how systems fit together, working out their edges and finding combinations that aren't obvious the first time you play. I wouldn't want every game I make to avoid that kind of depth.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    But I also started a card game I wanted to be able to play with my family. It was meant to be approachable enough that someone could sit down, learn the rhythm and get to the interesting part without spending the evening studying the resource economy.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    My first resource system had plenty to think about. You generated resources, spent them and recycled them. Recycled resources could give you utility or special effects. I liked how much choice I'd managed to put into that small part of the game. I was less pleased when I noticed how much of the rest of the game I'd had to keep simple to make room for it.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The easy conclusion would have been that the resource system was too complex. I think the more useful conclusion was that it was [VIOLET]**too expensive for the experience and audience I was designing for**[/VIOLET]. In a different game, that same kind of complexity might be a major selling point.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    [NOTE]***Quick note:*** When I say an audience, I don't mean sorting people into 'casual' and 'hardcore' boxes. I'm thinking about what players come to a particular game wanting to do, and how much effort they're happy to spend getting there.[/NOTE]
- type: heading-2
  text: "The right amount depends on what players came for"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I can enjoy a demanding strategy game on one evening and want something lighter on another. My tolerance for an intricate rules explanation has as much to do with why I'm playing as it does with my experience. Playing with friends, learning a competitive system, exploring a world and relaxing after work can all ask for different things.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    That's why I try to avoid treating complexity as a single number that every project should minimise. The question is what the complexity is *for*. Is working out the economy the main attraction? Is the game about reading an opponent several moves ahead? Is the system mostly there to support a story or a sense of movement?
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    If mastery of the economy is the fantasy, an elaborate economy may be exactly right. If I want people to concentrate on the performance, the economy should support that without demanding all their attention.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Neither player is wrong. Neither game is automatically better. They're promising different experiences.
- type: heading-2
  text: "There is complexity in the choice, and complexity around it"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I find it useful to separate the difficulty of making a decision from the difficulty of reaching it.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    A decision can be wonderfully difficult. I can see two useful moves, understand what each would do and still struggle to choose because one is safer while the other might set up something better. That's the kind of difficulty I often want to preserve.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The work surrounding that decision is different. If I need to remember three exceptions, check two piles, reconstruct what happened earlier and ask which step we're in before I can compare the moves, then much of the effort isn't strategic. It's administration.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    Sometimes administration is an intentional part of a simulation or a game aimed at people who enjoy that level of control. Often, though, it's just the price I've accidentally charged for reaching the interesting bit.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The distinction matters because a simpler interface doesn't have to mean a shallower game. I can show costs more clearly, make state visible or combine two procedures while leaving the underlying tradeoff intact. I haven't necessarily removed the thing that takes skill. I've made it easier to see.
- type: heading-2
  text: "Time is part of the design"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    There is another cost I try to remember: the time players have available. Learning a system over a long campaign is different from teaching it before a short match. A game people return to for hundreds of hours can gradually ask them to build up a vocabulary of interactions. A game they pick up occasionally needs to help them recover that understanding.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I can put a clever rule into a forty-minute game and be proud of it. If explaining the rule takes a significant part of the first session, I need to ask whether the payoff is strong enough. I also need to think about how often the rule actually matters. Something used every turn may become second nature. Something used once every few games can remain a constant reminder.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    This is especially noticeable when I'm playing with people who haven't spent as long inside the genre as I have. They don't bring all my assumptions to the table. If I rely on those assumptions, I may end up measuring familiarity rather than whether the mechanic is good.
- type: heading-2
  text: "An easy first move can lead somewhere deep"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    One of my favourite design goals is a system where a new player can make a reasonable first decision and an experienced player can see several decisions further ahead.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    You don't achieve that just by hiding rules. The base relationships have to be dependable. A move should do what it says. The player should be able to understand why something happened. Then the interesting difficulty can come from context: what the opponent might do, which opportunity they're sacrificing and how today's decision changes the next one.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    The same mechanic can be simple to perform and difficult to master. Moving a piece might take one action, but where it goes matters because of the board around it. Playing a card might require one clear cost, but deciding which card to play matters because of the resources and options that follow.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I like that kind of layered learning. The game doesn't have to explain its entire possibility space before someone is allowed to enjoy it. As players recognise the relationships, they can begin making better plans without learning a new set of exceptions every time.
- type: heading-2
  text: "Sometimes I would keep the complicated version"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I don't want this to become a rule that says I should cut anything that causes a new player to pause. Some games are built around discovery, experimentation and mastery of complicated systems. There are players who actively seek out that experience.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    If a system rewards the time spent understanding it, if its interactions remain consistent and if players enjoy the challenge it creates, I might choose to keep all that complexity. I'd still want to communicate it well. Complexity being intentional doesn't excuse unclear information or arbitrary exceptions.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    I'd also want the rest of the game to make room for it. A deep economy may call for a quieter turn structure. A demanding combat system may need progression that gives players time to practise its timing. Adding complexity everywhere at once doesn't automatically give players more depth.
- type: heading-2
  text: "What I ask before simplifying"
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    When a playtest feels overwhelming, I try not to immediately cross out rules. I first want to know what players were trying to pay attention to, where they lost track and whether they were enjoying the difficult parts.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    If they're making thoughtful choices but struggling to read the consequences, I should work on communication. If they understand everything but the decisions feel automatic, I may need better tension. If they're spending most of the session following procedures they don't care about, I need to question the systems themselves.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    That is a much more useful diagnosis than 'make it simpler'. It gives me a direction while respecting the kind of game I'm actually trying to create.
- type: paragraph
  text: |-
    [BLOCK]I don't think every game needs less complexity. I think complexity should earn the player's attention. The right question is whether the experience they get back is worth what the game asks them to learn, remember and manage.[/BLOCK]
